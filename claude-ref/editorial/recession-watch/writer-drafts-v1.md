> recession at this stage on depth, narrowing on breadth.

### The template it fills from

> [Output] is [essentially at / down X% from] its [peak month] peak with [Y]% of
> industries below their own peaks -- [clear of / within / beyond] every past
> recession at this stage.

Three-state classifier word (§1.4 joint read, §6 wording):
- **clear of** -- the current track is outside the past-recession range on at
  least one of depth / breadth (resting state).
- **within** -- inside the past-recession range on depth AND breadth AND
  duration jointly.
- **beyond** -- deeper, broader, and longer than the median past recession at
  this stage.

Note for the build: at the current reading the at-peak phrasing ("essentially
at its peak") is the honest render -- a -0.001% drawdown is not "down 0.0%."
When a genuine drawdown opens, the template's "down X% from" branch takes over.

## SURFACE 3 — Methodology / Comparators / Sources (read-once register)

### What we measure

We track a downturn the way the C.D. Howe Business Cycle Council judges one:
not on a single number, but on how deep it runs, how broadly it spreads, and
how long it lasts. Four readings, two for output and two for jobs.

- **Output depth** -- how far real GDP has fallen below its most recent peak,
  in per cent.
- **Output breadth** -- the share of industries whose output is below its own
  peak.
- **Employment depth** -- how far employment has fallen below its peak.
- **Employment breadth** -- the share of sectors shedding jobs since the
  employment peak.

A real recession shows up on all four at once: output and jobs, the size of
the fall and its spread. No single reading is the verdict; the verdict is the
joint read.

### The cycle-peak clock

Every line is measured forward from its own cycle peak -- the month output (or
employment) stood highest before it turned down. The horizontal axis counts
months since that peak, so today's downturn and the recessions of 1981, 1990,
2008, and 2020 all begin at the same origin and can be read at the same stage.
We measure from the peak, not the calendar, so that every downturn sits on the
same clock.

### The comparators

The benchmarks are the four Canadian recessions since 1981 -- 1981-82, 1990-92,
2008-09, and 2020 -- as dated by the C.D. Howe Business Cycle Council. The
1974-75 recession is left out: the monthly industry data the breadth readings
need begins later, and the pre-1981 vintage is not built on today's industry
classification. StatCan's monthly GDP by industry switched to its current
classification around 1997, so the 1981 and 1990 breadth lines rest on a series
that is spliced across that seam -- we flag it plainly rather than hide it.

### A monitor, not a forecast

This page reports where the economy sits today against past recessions at the
same stage. It does not forecast whether a recession will arrive, and it does
not date one -- only the C.D. Howe Business Cycle Council does that, and it does
so in arrears, often more than a year after the fact. A line entering the
past-recession range is a reason to watch, not a call.

### A note on revisions

Monthly GDP by industry and the Labour Force Survey are both revised. Today's
downturn is drawn on provisional data and its lines will shift as StatCan
revises; the four past recessions are shown on final, settled vintages.

## PAGE ABSTRACT (under the header question)

Header question (from brief §1.1): "Is Canada in a recession -- and how would we
know before the committee calls it?"

> Not on the evidence so far, but the all-clear isn't clean. Output is sitting
> right at its February peak and the job market has barely softened, which keeps
> this well clear of any past recession on the size of the fall. The unease is in
> the spread: more than half of industries are already off their own peaks, broader
> than the shallow output drop alone would suggest. We watch all of it the way the
> recession daters do -- depth, breadth, and duration together, against the four
> downturns Canada has actually had.
