# BuildForce Canada — primary-source verification

Author: research agent
Date: 2026-05-25
Purpose: Decide whether Sibley Creek can cite BuildForce Canada as the supply-side baseline in a forthcoming research piece on whether Canada has the skilled-trades workforce to execute the Carney government's Build Canada agenda.

---

## 1. TL;DR

BuildForce Canada is **the** canonical Canadian source for ten-year construction-trades labour supply-and-demand forecasts. It is a national, industry-led non-profit (founded April 1, 2013 as the successor to the federally-funded Construction Sector Council, which itself ran 2001–2013); funded by industry contributions plus project-specific federal/provincial grants; governed by a 15-member board with broad construction-industry representation (contractor associations, building-trades unions, owner/client companies). Its core flagship is the annual **Construction and Maintenance Looking Forward** (CMLF) suite — one national and ten provincial reports — released each spring on a fixed cycle. The most recent national edition (2025–2034) was released April 4, 2025; the next national edition is on track for spring 2026. Forecast modelling is done in partnership with **Prism Economics and Analysis** (Ottawa/Toronto), with regional LMI committees validating numbers province by province. Outputs are **free and publicly accessible** at `constructionforecasts.ca` and `buildforce.ca`. It is widely cited by industry associations (CCA, CHBA), trade press, federal Job Bank (ESDC), and at least one academic critical-review article in *Canadian Journal of Civil Engineering*; the federal "1.4 million additional trades workers by 2033" figure used by the Carney government in its April 29, 2026 Team Canada Strong announcement is **not explicitly attributed** to BuildForce in the speech but is broadly consistent with BuildForce's all-trades hiring requirement when residential + non-residential and replacement + growth demand are stacked. **Verdict: defensible primary source with caveats** — cite for supply/demand framing and trade-level granularity, but be aware that (a) Prism Economics, not BuildForce staff, owns the model, (b) major-project demand is built up from a stakeholder-validated project list rather than a bottom-up cost-to-hours conversion, and (c) productivity assumptions are not explicitly modelled — meaning Sibley will need to overlay its own productivity / modular-construction view if that is the editorial point.

## 2. Organization

**Legal form.** National non-profit, industry-led. Officially launched April 1, 2013. Head office at 280 Albert Street, Suite 700, Ottawa.

**History.** BuildForce Canada is the direct successor to the **Construction Sector Council (CSC)**, which operated 2001–2013 under the federal Sector Council Program at Human Resources and Skills Development Canada (HRSDC, the predecessor of ESDC). When HRSDC informed the sector councils in 2011 that core federal funding would be phased out by March 2013, construction-industry leaders incorporated BuildForce as a successor entity — same mandate, new funding model, new governance.

- Predecessor history: [BuildForce Canada replaces the Construction Sector Council — REMI Network](https://www.reminetwork.com/articles/buildforce-canada-replaces-csc/)
- Transition mechanics: [Work of CSC to continue through BuildForce Canada — HPAC Magazine](https://www.hpacmag.com/heating-plumbing-air-conditioning-general/work-of-construction-sector-council-to-continue-through-buildforce-canada/1002252390/)

**Funding.** Operational funding from private-sector industry contributions; project-specific funding sought from federal and provincial governments. The shift away from federal core funding is the central reason BuildForce exists as a separate entity from the old CSC. (Source: REMI Network, ibid.) Industry partner organizations represented on the board include the Canadian Construction Association, Canadian Home Builders' Association, Canada's Building Trades Unions, CLAC, Progressive Contractors Association of Canada, Independent Contractors and Businesses Association, the provincial Building and Construction Trades councils, and corporate members (Shell Canada, Suncor Energy, BC Hydro, Irving Oil).

**Governance — board composition (2026).** Source: [Board of Directors — BuildForce Canada](https://www.buildforce.ca/en/board-of-directors/). Fifteen members, with a deliberate balance between **employer-side** (contractor associations, owner-client corporates) and **labour-side** (building-trades unions, council representatives). My recount of the affiliations on the official page:

- **Employer / contractor associations (5):** Paul de Jong (PCA Canada), Nicole Emmett (CCA), Chris Gardner (ICBA), Kevin Lee (CHBA), Wayne Prins (CLAC — note: CLAC is a non-traditional labour organization but functions partly as an employer alternative to the building-trades model).
- **Owner / client corporates (4):** Graham Polischuk (Shell Canada, Vice Chair), Jeff Acland (BC Hydro), Cole Carson (Irving Oil), Chris Woloshyn (Suncor Energy).
- **Building trades / labour (5):** Sean Strickland (Canada's Building Trades Unions, Past Chair), Marc Arsenault (Provincial Building & Construction Trades Council of Ontario), Brynn Bourke (BC Building Trades Council), Terry Parker (Building Trades of Alberta), plus one of the CLR seats.
- **Construction Labour Relations associations (2):** Warren Douglas (CLR Saskatchewan, Chair), Terry French (CLR Newfoundland and Labrador). CLR associations are employer bargaining agents under provincial labour codes, so functionally employer-side.

Net read: **balanced, not labour-dominated**. The Chair seat sits with an employer bargaining agent (CLR Saskatchewan), the Vice-Chair with an owner-client corporate (Shell Canada), and the Past Chair with building trades (Strickland, CBTU). Owner-client corporates have meaningful weight (4 of 15), which matters because they fund big projects and are exposed to labour-availability risk on the demand side.

**Headcount.** Not publicly disclosed. BuildForce describes itself as a small national secretariat that contracts modelling and analysis out to Prism Economics. Likely fewer than 20 staff.

**Leadership change in 2026 — flag.** Bill Ferreira, executive director since August 2017, departed in early 2026 to lead the Ottawa Construction Association. **Irwin Bess** started as the new BuildForce executive director on March 2, 2026 (eight weeks before this verification). Bess gave a Daily Commercial News interview in March 2026 emphasizing "next era of data integration." Worth tracking — a new ED can change publication cadence and methodology priorities.

- Leadership change: [New BuildForce executive director Bess contemplates next era of data integration — Daily Commercial News](https://canada.constructconnect.com/dcn/news/economic/2026/03/new-buildforce-executive-director-bess-contemplates-next-era-of-data-integration)
- Bess appointment announcement: [Bess announced as new BuildForce Canada executive director — Daily Commercial News](https://canada.constructconnect.com/dcn/news/labour/2026/02/bess-announced-as-new-buildforce-canada-executive-director)

## 3. Publications

### Core annual product: Construction and Maintenance Looking Forward (CMLF)

Released each spring as a coordinated suite:

- **National report** — one document, ten-year forecast horizon, all provinces aggregated.
- **Ten provincial summary reports** — one per province; Yukon/NWT/Nunavut covered at lower granularity in the territorial summary.
- Most recent national edition: **2025–2034 Construction and Maintenance Looking Forward**, released April 4, 2025. Source: [Renewed residential activity and ongoing work on major non-residential projects elevate construction demands to 2034 — BuildForce Canada press release](https://www.buildforce.ca/en/press-release/renewed-residential-activity-and-ongoing-work-on-major-non-residential-projects-elevate-construction-demands-to-2034/).
- Prior national edition: **2024–2033 CMLF**, released March 28, 2024. Source: [Canada's Construction Sector Is Poised To Grow Through 2033](https://www.buildforce.ca/en/press-release/canadas-construction-sector-is-poised-to-grow-through-2033/).
- Spring 2026 cycle: at the time of this verification (May 25, 2026), BuildForce has continued its monthly LFS-tracking blog cadence (most recent post May 25, 2026 — "April LFS finds notable contractions among the eldest and youngest components of the construction labour force") but I did not find a press-release announcement of a 2026–2035 CMLF release as of this date. Likely either in late spring or pushed to the new executive director's timeline. Worth a direct check before publishing.

### Other publications

- **Construction Forecasts portal** — `constructionforecasts.ca` — the interactive front end where ten-year demand and supply numbers can be browsed by province, sub-region, sector (residential / non-residential / engineering / maintenance), and individual trade/occupation. **Public, free, no login.** Source: [Resource Centre — Construction Forecasts](https://www.constructionforecasts.ca/en/highlights).
- **Construction Key Indicators** — free monthly LFS-based industry snapshot.
- **Forecast Summary Reports** — provincial PDFs, free download.
- **Research Reports** — periodic deep-dives on diversity, productivity, immigration, residential workforce, etc. Free.
- **Labour Market Information Dashboard** — visual interface for the forecast data. Free.
- **Respectful Workplaces Toolkit, productivity tools, e-learning** — workforce-development products, not labour-economics outputs. Free.
- **BuildForce Magazine** — newsletter / editorial. Free.

### Paywall structure

**No paywalled products were identified.** All forecast outputs at the level a researcher would need for a Sibley deep-dive appear to be public.

### Trades covered

- **34 construction trades and occupations** covered in the supply-and-demand model.
- Includes the major Red Seal construction trades (carpenters, electricians, plumbers, sheet metal workers, ironworkers, refrigeration and air conditioning mechanics, welders, heavy-duty equipment technicians, bricklayers, etc.) and key construction occupations that are not formal trades (construction managers, civil engineers in construction, construction estimators, supervisors).
- Source: [About The Data — BuildForce Canada](https://www.buildforce.ca/en/about-the-data/) — "more than 30 trades and occupations by province."

### Sector coverage

- **Residential** construction (single, multi-family, renovation).
- **Non-residential** construction — split further into industrial, commercial, and institutional (ICI).
- **Engineering construction** (heavy infrastructure — roads, bridges, transit, utilities, energy, pipelines).
- **Maintenance** of installed industrial capacity.

### Coverage gaps for a Build-Canada analysis

- Does NOT cover non-construction trades that may still be Build-Canada-relevant: industrial millwrights deployed in nuclear/manufacturing settings, mining trades, manufacturing trades. For a Build-Canada piece touching nuclear new-build or critical-minerals processing, BuildForce is only part of the picture — pair with Canadian Apprenticeship Forum and Statistics Canada COPS projections for cross-sectoral trades.
- Maintenance of *existing* installed capacity (e.g., oil sands turnaround work, refinery maintenance) is included on the demand side, which is unusually thorough — this is a strength worth highlighting.

## 4. Methodology

### Model architecture

The forecast is produced by **Prism Economics and Analysis** under contract to BuildForce. Prism is a private economic consultancy (Toronto/Ottawa). Source: [Labour Market Analysis and Forecasting — Prism Economics](https://www.prismeconomics.com/labour-market-analysis-and-forecasting): "Prism Economics leads the supply-side forecasting, market assessment and report writing for BuildForce Canada (formerly known as the Construction Sector Council)."

The methodology builds on earlier work by the **Construction Owners Association of Alberta (COAA)** and the **Commission de la construction du Québec (CCQ)**. Source: [About The Data — BuildForce Canada](https://www.buildforce.ca/en/about-the-data/).

### Demand side

1. **Macro economic outlook** — investment paths for residential and non-residential construction by province, drawing on Statistics Canada's investment-intentions data, CMHC housing-starts projections, and a stakeholder-validated **list of major projects** (megaprojects in resource, energy, transit, utilities, healthcare, education).
2. **Investment-to-employment translation** — investment $ flows are translated into person-hours and then peak-period and annual-average employment by trade. This is the proprietary core of the Prism model. Cost-to-labour ratios are validated by regional LMI committees.
3. **Major project demand** is layered onto the baseline by phasing each known project against its construction schedule. This is a strength for Build-Canada analysis: when a megaproject (e.g., a nuclear refurbishment, an LNG facility, a transit project) shifts schedule, the model can be re-run.
4. Output is **peak workforce by year**, not person-year equivalents — this matters because Build-Canada projects all peaking simultaneously is the worst case, and the model reveals that pattern when it exists.

### Supply side

1. **Stock** — current registered workforce in each trade by province, sourced from Statistics Canada LFS (Table 14-10-0022-01 and related), supplemented by apprenticeship registration data.
2. **Net additions** — apprenticeship completions (Red Seal Program tracking), new entrants under age 30, immigration inflows, interprovincial in-migration.
3. **Net subtractions** — retirements (age-cohort modelled; 2025–2034 forecast assumes ~270,000 retirements nationally, roughly 20% of the 2024 workforce), attrition out of the trade, interprovincial out-migration.
4. **Immigration** — the 2025–2034 forecast embeds the federal immigration-levels plan: "Canada is projected to welcome nearly 4.4 million new immigrants between 2025 and 2034, with newcomers currently comprising about 20% of construction's labour force nationally." (Source: 2025–2034 CMLF press release.)
5. **Interprovincial mobility** is modelled at the regional-committee level — Prism's process explicitly considers "intra-regional and inter-provincial mobility" (Source: [Prism Economics — Labour Market Analysis and Forecasting](https://www.prismeconomics.com/labour-market-analysis-and-forecasting)).

### Time phasing and granularity

- **Ten-year horizon**, updated annually. Current published horizon: 2025 through 2034.
- **Annual** granularity, with peak-period (typically summer construction season) employment identified.
- **Provincial** granularity in the public CMLF reports; **sub-regional** (e.g., five Ontario regions) granularity available through the `constructionforecasts.ca` portal.

### Productivity assumptions

**Productivity changes are not explicitly modelled as a separate driver.** The investment-to-labour-hours ratio is held roughly stable over the forecast horizon, with adjustments coming through stakeholder validation rather than an econometric productivity equation. This is the most consequential methodological limitation for Build-Canada analysis: if modular / prefab / off-site construction or AI-assisted design were to materially shift productivity, BuildForce's labour demand projections would overstate on-site trades requirements. Conversely, the unexplained Canadian residential-construction productivity decline over the past two decades is not endogenized either, so the supply-shortfall framing may understate the demand-side problem if that decline persists.

For Sibley: this is exactly where an editorial overlay is justified. The supply-side baseline is BuildForce; the productivity sensitivity is Sibley's value-add.

### Validation

Outputs are validated by **regional LMI committees** representing labour, contractors, owner-clients, and federal/provincial government training/education providers. Each committee meets at minimum annually; some quarterly. (Source: [About The Data — BuildForce Canada](https://www.buildforce.ca/en/about-the-data/).)

## 5. Citation footprint

### Government use

- **Job Bank / ESDC** — the Job Bank's **Sectoral Profile: Construction (Atlantic Region 2024)** cites BuildForce projections directly in its labour-market trend analysis. Source: [Sector Profile - Construction: Atlantic Region 2024 — Job Bank](https://www.jobbank.gc.ca/trend-analysis/job-market-reports/atlantic-region/sectoral-profile-construction). This is direct federal-government use.
- **Statistics Canada** — StatCan is a primary *input* to BuildForce (LFS Table 14-10-0022-01 is the workforce-stock data source) but does not itself cite BuildForce in standard releases. StatCan and BuildForce are complementary not competing.
- **Finance Canada Fall Economic Statements / Budgets** — neither the 2023 FES, 2024 FES, nor Budget 2025 documents I checked explicitly cite BuildForce by name in their construction-labour discussions. They reference "industry projections" and "labour shortages" without an attribution chain.
- **PBO Build Canada Homes report (RP-2526-020-S, fall 2025)** — does NOT cite BuildForce. The PBO report focuses on housing-program funding mechanics and unit-production estimates, not labour-supply constraints. Source: [Build Canada Homes and the Outlook for Housing Programs under Budget 2025 — PBO](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2526-020-S--build-canada-homes-outlook-housing-programs-under-budget-2025--maisons-canada-perspectives-entourant-programmes-logement-dans-cadre-budget-2025).
- **Carney "1.4 million additional trades workers by 2033" claim (April 29, 2026)** — the PM's official speech and the related press release do NOT cite BuildForce. The figure appears to be a federal Finance/ESDC composite across **all** trades (not just construction), since BuildForce's all-construction-only hiring requirement to 2034 is ~380,500 (growth + replacement). The 1.4M figure is roughly 3.7x BuildForce's number, which is consistent with construction trades being roughly a quarter of all-economy skilled-trades demand (skilled trades broadly include manufacturing, transportation, automotive service, industrial maintenance, etc.). Source: [Prime Minister Carney announces Team Canada Strong — PM's office](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/speeches/2026/04/29/prime-minister-carney-announces-team-canada-strong-nationwide-plan-recruit). **For Sibley:** do not represent the 1.4M figure as a BuildForce number. If you cite both, treat them as complementary scopes (BuildForce = construction trades only; the federal 1.4M = all skilled trades).

### Industry use

- **Canadian Home Builders' Association (CHBA)** — directly cites BuildForce projections in its workforce-supply communications, including the widely-reported "residential construction workforce must double to meet CMHC's 5.8M housing-starts target" framing. Source: [Monumental workforce increase required to meet housing supply targets — CHBA](https://www.chba.ca/2024/04/11/monumental-workforce-increase-required-to-meet-housing-supply-targets-to-address-affordability-in-canada/).
- **Canadian Construction Association (CCA)** — represented on the BuildForce board; treats BuildForce data as the industry's authoritative supply-and-demand source.
- **Trade press** (Daily Commercial News, Journal of Commerce, ReNew Canada, On-Site Magazine, Plumbing & HVAC, Electrical Business, Construction Canada, Link2Build, Building Excellence, ConstructConnect) — uniformly treat BuildForce CMLF release days as a tier-1 industry event and reproduce its numbers without challenging them.
- **Provincial construction associations** (OGCA, ICBA, BC Building Trades, etc.) — cite BuildForce as the primary forecasting source.

### Academic use

- The 2024 critical review in the **Canadian Journal of Civil Engineering** ("Strategies to alleviate Canada's impending construction labour shortage: a critical review") includes BuildForce among its authoritative sources alongside the Canadian Apprenticeship Forum and Statistics Canada. Source: [Strategies to alleviate Canada's impending construction labour shortage — CJCE](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1139/cjce-2023-0486).
- The 2020 **Social Research and Demonstration Corporation (SRDC)** stakeholder evaluation (BuildForce-sponsored) is the most formal independent assessment, though its public abstract does not surface adverse findings. Source: [Evaluation of BuildForce Canada's LMI Program — SRDC](https://www.srdc.org/project/evaluation-of-buildforce-canadas-labour-market-information-lmi-program/).

### Bank-economist / think-tank use

- I did not find C.D. Howe, IRPP, or Fraser Institute reports that cite BuildForce as authority. This is a gap — these think-tanks more often source from Statistics Canada or commission their own analysis. The implication is that BuildForce is treated as an **industry-trusted** rather than **policy-research-establishment-default** source. For Sibley, citing BuildForce alongside StatCan/CMHC is the right register; citing it as if it were CDH would understate its specificity but is not wrong.

## 6. Most recent forecast — headline numbers

Source for this section unless otherwise noted: **2025–2034 Construction and Maintenance Looking Forward**, released April 4, 2025. [BuildForce Canada press release](https://www.buildforce.ca/en/press-release/renewed-residential-activity-and-ongoing-work-on-major-non-residential-projects-elevate-construction-demands-to-2034/).

### Industry baseline (2024)
- Construction employs **1.6 million Canadians** (~1 in 13 workers).
- Contributes **7% of Canada's national GDP**.
- Current labour force ~1.73 million; ~1.65 million actively employed (Source: [Age-related attrition drives construction employment and labour-force metrics lower again in February 2026 — BuildForce blog](https://www.buildforce.ca/en/blog/age-related-attrition-drives-construction-employment-and-labour-force-metrics-lower-again-in-february-2026/)).

### Headline labour-force math, 2025–2034
- **Growth in demand** requires the labour force to expand by **111,600 workers**.
- **Projected retirements:** ~**270,000 experienced workers** (≈ 20% of 2024 workforce).
- **Total hiring requirement:** **380,500 workers by 2034.**
- **Projected first-time entrants under 30:** ~272,200.
- **Net structural shortfall** at the end of the forecast horizon, even with current recruitment trends: up to **108,300 workers**.

### Sector split
- **Residential** sector employment projected to grow 6% above 2024 levels by 2034. Path: largely flat in 2025, then steady increases 2026–2034 as interest-rate pressure eases and pent-up demand returns.
- **Non-residential** sector employment projected to grow 8% above 2024 levels by 2034. Path: peaks in 2027, then ebbs with major-project completion schedules.

### Provincial highlights
- **Ontario** — projected **154,100 new construction workers** required by 2034. Projected shortfall ~52,000 by 2034. Source: [BuildForce Canada outlook projects shortfall of 52,000 workers in Ontario by 2034 — Link2Build](https://link2build.ca/news/articles/2025/april/buildforce-canada-outlook-projects-shortfall-of-52-000-workers-in-ontario-by-2034/).
- **Alberta** — non-residential construction employment projected 8% above 2024 levels by 2026; needs to replace **>43,000 retiring workers**.
- **Manitoba** — needs ~19,400 new construction workers by 2034 (per BuildForce, as cited by CBC).
- BuildForce publishes the full province-by-province breakdown in the provincial summary reports.

### Residential-only workforce stress (2024–2033 cycle, still the canonical reference for housing-target analysis)
- To deliver the **5.8 million new homes by 2033** scenario implied by CMHC's housing-affordability assessment, BuildForce projected the **residential construction workforce would have to grow to over 1,030,000** — an **83% increase** above the 2023 baseline. This is BuildForce's own framing, widely cited by CHBA and industry as the strongest evidence that the housing target is workforce-constrained. Source: [Residential construction workforce needs to double — Daily Commercial News](https://canada.constructconnect.com/dcn/news/economic/2024/04/residential-construction-workforce-needs-to-double-buildforce).

### Time horizon
- Ten years from the release year. Current published horizon: **2025–2034**.

## 7. Limitations and where Sibley would need to extend

1. **Productivity is not endogenously modelled.** Investment-to-labour-hours ratios are held roughly stable. If Sibley's editorial point is that modular/prefab/off-site construction could close some of the labour gap, that overlay must come from outside BuildForce (CMHC's productivity research, Statistics Canada's industry productivity tables, or original Sibley analysis).
2. **Non-construction trades not covered.** Industrial millwrights deployed in nuclear new-build, mining trades, manufacturing trades — BuildForce stops at the construction-industry boundary. For a full Build-Canada-pace analysis covering nuclear SMRs, critical-minerals processing, or upstream manufacturing for transmission infrastructure, pair BuildForce with Canadian Apprenticeship Forum data and Statistics Canada's COPS occupational projections.
3. **Major-project demand depends on a stakeholder-validated project list.** The model is only as forward-looking as the project pipeline shared with the regional committees. Politically-announced megaprojects (e.g., a Pickering refurbishment cost overrun, an unannounced LNG expansion, a delayed transit line) that are not in the validated project list will not appear in the demand path until the next annual cycle. **For a Build-Canada piece, Sibley's own project-level demand stack is the value-add** — BuildForce supplies the supply-side baseline; Sibley overlays a Build-Big project list and shows the peak-demand stack against BuildForce's supply trajectory.
4. **Federal "1.4M trades by 2033" attribution is loose.** The Carney April 29, 2026 announcement does not cite BuildForce or any other source. If Sibley wants to anchor against the federal number, it must reconstruct the math itself or treat the federal claim as a political talking point rather than a verified projection. BuildForce's ~380,500 figure for construction trades specifically is the cleaner number to cite as a primary projection.
5. **Productivity decline in residential construction is ignored.** Canada's residential construction productivity has declined materially over the past two decades (see Statistics Canada productivity-by-industry tables and BoC staff working-paper coverage). BuildForce's investment-to-labour ratio assumes status-quo productivity, which arguably **understates** the labour requirement to deliver any housing-starts target. This cuts the other way from limitation (1) — modular could help; the past two decades' trend hurts.
6. **Independent academic critique is thin.** The 2024 *Canadian Journal of Civil Engineering* review and the 2020 SRDC evaluation are the two formal external assessments I found. Neither is a methodological audit of the Prism model. BuildForce's forecast accuracy against ex-post outcomes does not appear to be publicly tracked. If Sibley wants to claim "BuildForce has historically over/under-forecast by X," it would need to do that legwork itself by retrieving the 2014–2023 and 2015–2024 CMLFs from web archives and comparing to LFS realized outcomes.
7. **Leadership transition in 2026 is fresh.** New executive director Irwin Bess started March 2, 2026. Methodology and publication cadence under new leadership not yet established at scale. Worth checking back in early 2027 before treating the 2026–2035 CMLF as a guaranteed annual release.

## 8. Bottom line

**Defensible primary source — with caveats.**

BuildForce Canada is the right baseline for the supply side of any Canadian construction-trades workforce analysis. The data is publicly accessible, the methodology is documented (if not exhaustively), the model has been continuously run since 2005 (CSC era plus BuildForce era), the modelling partner (Prism Economics) is a credible independent consultancy, and the outputs are used by ESDC's Job Bank, the major industry associations, the trade press, and at least one peer-reviewed academic critical review. The board is balanced rather than captured by either employer or labour side.

For Sibley, the defensible move is to cite BuildForce explicitly — by report name (Construction and Maintenance Looking Forward 2025–2034) and by URL — for any claim about the construction-trades workforce baseline, retirement wave, immigration inflow, provincial breakdowns, or trade-level granularity. The four caveats Sibley should keep on the desk while writing:

1. The federal "1.4M trades workers by 2033" figure used by the Carney government is **not** BuildForce's number. BuildForce's all-construction equivalent is ~380,500 to 2034. If Sibley wants to push back on the federal number's framing, this is the cleanest cite.
2. BuildForce holds productivity roughly stable. Any thesis about productivity (modular up, residential-productivity decline down) is a Sibley overlay, not a BuildForce claim.
3. BuildForce's major-project demand depends on the stakeholder project list it has at the time of forecast. A Build-Canada-pace piece's value-add is its own bottom-up project-level demand stack laid against BuildForce's supply trajectory.
4. BuildForce does not cover non-construction trades. For nuclear new-build, mining, or manufacturing trades, pair with Canadian Apprenticeship Forum + StatCan COPS.

The publication can credibly use phrasing along the lines of *"BuildForce Canada — the construction industry's national workforce-forecasting body — projects ..."* without overstatement. Avoid phrasing that implies it is a government agency (it is not) or that it is the source for the Carney "1.4M" figure (it is not).
