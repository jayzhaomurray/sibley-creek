## Task Brief

Jay asked for more than a list of "top consensus views." The useful object is a consensus map:

1. What do Canadian economists and market participants broadly agree on?
2. What themes are repeated across the Bank of Canada, private-sector forecasters, bank desks, and policy institutions?

## Executive Synthesis

The Canadian economics consensus in late May 2026 is not bullish, bearish, or crisis-oriented. It is a **managed-adjustment consensus**.

The common view is:

> Canada has absorbed the first year of tariff and geopolitical shocks better than feared; growth is weak but not recessionary; inflation is temporarily lifted by energy but should return toward 2%; the Bank of Canada is probably on hold through 2026; labour is soft but not breaking; public spending and infrastructure help offset private-sector weakness; productivity and business investment are the real medium-term problems; trade uncertainty is still the dominant external risk.

That consensus is coherent. It is also vulnerable because it relies on several comfortable bridges:

1. Investment recovers enough to offset structural trade damage.
2. AI/productivity optimism can be discussed without proving Canada has the private-sector adoption engine to capture it.
3. Retail-sales / consumer-resilience readings can stand in for true household demand.
4. BoC communication is treated as a near-meeting reaction function, not a 6-12 month policy regime.
5. Canadian equities are often discussed through valuation and sector mix rather than corporate-profit generation.

The Sibley Creek opportunity is not to say "consensus is too bullish" or "consensus is too bearish." It is to say:

> Consensus is treating Canada as cyclically soft but structurally repairable. The risk is that the repair mechanism itself is missing.

## What Exactly Is The Consensus?

### 1. Growth: slow, below potential, but not recession

Consensus: Canada grows around 1%-1.6% in 2026 and closer to 1.7%-1.9% in 2027. Recession risk exists but is not the base case.

Evidence:

- The Bank of Canada Market Participants Survey median expected Q4/Q4 real GDP growth of 1.6% by end-2026 and 1.9% by end-2027.
- The federal Spring Economic Update private-sector survey put annual real GDP growth at 1.1% in 2026 and 1.9% in 2027.
- TD's May 2026 tables showed Canada at 1.1% in 2026 and 1.7% in 2027.
- RBC's May 2026 detail showed annual growth of 1.2% in 2026 and 1.5% in 2027.
- S&P Global Ratings forecast 1.4% in 2026 and 1.7% in 2027.

Theme underneath: **muddle-through growth.** The economy is weak enough to leave excess supply, but not weak enough to force a recession narrative.

Attackable angle: the consensus is less about growth strength than about growth composition. The interesting question is whether the assumed rotation into investment/export/public-infrastructure growth is real.

### 2. Output gap: almost everyone agrees Canada is below potential

Consensus: Canada has excess supply.

Evidence:

- In the BoC Market Participants Survey, 96.3% of respondents said current GDP is below potential; 0% said it is above potential.

Theme underneath: **inflation pressure should be containable because slack exists.**

Attackable angle: the output-gap consensus is powerful but lazy. It may obscure sectoral capacity constraints, especially skilled trades, housing construction, energy/infrastructure, and AI/data-centre-related power constraints.

### 3. Inflation: near-term oil lift, then back toward 2%

Consensus: CPI is lifted in 2026 by energy/geopolitics but returns toward the 2% target in 2027.

Evidence:

- Market participants' median CPI forecast was 2.6% at end-2026, 2.1% at end-2027, and 2.0% five years ahead.
- Spring Economic Update private-sector economists expected CPI at 2.5% in 2026, 1.9% in 2027, and 2.0% from 2028 onward.
- The BoC April MPR expected inflation to slow to about 2.5% in June and return to 2% in early 2027 if oil declines as assumed.

Theme underneath: **inflation is a shock-management problem, not a new regime problem.**

Attackable angle: the consensus assumes limited pass-through from oil/supply-chain changes and enough demand weakness to absorb cost shocks. Sibley Creek can test where pass-through is actually appearing and whether the BoC is underestimating persistence in selected components.

### 4. Bank of Canada: hold through 2026, then gradual rise

Consensus: The BoC is done cutting and likely holds at 2.25% through 2026, with a gradual move higher in 2027 as growth firms.

Evidence:

- In the BoC Market Participants Survey, the median policy-rate path was 2.25% for every 2026 meeting, 2.50% by March 2027, and 2.75% by Q4 2027.
- The Spring Economic Update private-sector survey said the BoC keeps the policy rate at 2.25% through 2026, with gradual increases from early 2027.
- BMO argued the Bank's current hold will last a long while, despite market pricing for near-term hikes.

Theme underneath: **policy is in a neutral holding pattern.**

Attackable angle: Fred's point matters here. The shops are too focused on the next decision. The wedge is not "June hold or hike." It is "what 2027 policy regime is being priced incorrectly?"

### 5. Labour: soft landing, not collapse

Consensus: The unemployment rate is elevated but should drift down as growth stabilizes.

Evidence:

- The Spring Economic Update private-sector survey expected unemployment to average 6.5% in 2026 and trend to 6.0% by 2029.
- TD forecast unemployment of 6.6% in 2026 and 6.3% in 2027.
- RBC expected unemployment of 6.5% in 2026 and 6.0% in 2027.
- The BoC Business Outlook Survey found hiring intentions had recovered from weak levels, though planned staffing increases were expected to be small.

Theme underneath: **labour is weak but not recessionary.**

Attackable angle: labour consensus often treats LFS as the central pulse. A data-construction note on LFS vs SEPH, and why SEPH matters for GDP/payroll income, is a clean Sibley Creek wedge.

### 6. Consumption: modest, constrained, but supported

Consensus: Consumption grows slowly because population growth is subdued and households are squeezed, but fiscal transfers, past rate cuts, and labour-income stability prevent a sharp break.

Evidence:

- The BoC April MPR forecast consumption growth to average just above 1% over the projection horizon because population growth remains subdued.
- Desjardins argued fuel-tax relief and GST/HST-credit expansion should offset some drag from higher energy prices.
- RBC and TD both forecast modest consumption growth in 2026.

Theme underneath: **consumer spending is tired, but not broken.**

Attackable angle: retail sales is the wrong shorthand for consumption. A strong note would show why retail-sales excitement misses services, population effects, real income, and the national-accounts construction.

### 7. Trade: uncertainty persists, but the worst fear has faded

Consensus: Trade tensions remain a risk, but firms have adapted somewhat; CUSMA exemptions and adjustment reduce near-term panic.

Evidence:

- BoC BOS said fewer firms reported trade tensions affecting expected sales/costs and fewer exporters were hesitant to enter the US market.
- The same BOS found little evidence of material non-US export diversification.
- Market participants' top upside risk was easing trade tensions; top downside risks included increasing geopolitical and trade tensions.

Theme underneath: **tariff shock has become a chronic headwind, not an acute crisis.**

Attackable angle: trade uncertainty is becoming a stale consensus topic. The better question is not "trade uncertainty bad"; it is "what productive capacity or investment decision has actually changed?"

### 8. Investment/productivity: everyone agrees it is the medium-term problem

Consensus: Productivity and business investment are Canada's core structural weakness, but many outlooks assume some improvement as public investment, infrastructure, AI adoption, and adjustment to tariffs lift medium-term growth.

Evidence:

- IMF said weak productivity is a key challenge and called for reforms supporting investment, competition, and innovation.
- StatCan summarized Canada's productivity problem as hampered for decades by weak business investment, declining R&D, stalling patents, and lagging intangibles.
- BoC BOS said investment intentions improved, especially among firms unaffected by trade tensions.
- The BoC April MPR projected growth in exports and business investment to resume on a lower trajectory.

Theme underneath: **Canada's medium-term problem is productivity, but the consensus still assumes a repair path.**

Attackable angle: this is probably the cleanest Sibley Creek target. The consensus says investment rebounds. The question is: from what engine? Public capex is not the same as private-sector capital deepening, and AI adoption is not the same as an AI profit cycle.

### 9. AI: acknowledged as a productivity hope, not yet a proven Canadian growth engine

Consensus: AI could help productivity, but adoption is uneven and uncertain.

Evidence:

- Bank of Canada argued AI could affect productivity, growth, employment and inflation, and could lower business costs and support wages.
- StatCan said AI adoption is tied to complementary capabilities such as R&D, cloud computing, data analytics, robotics, and ICT training.
- StatCan also stressed Canada's persistent productivity challenge and weak business investment.

Theme underneath: **AI is the upside optionality in the productivity story.**

Attackable angle: Fred's sharper formulation is better: Canada does not have an AI landscape comparable to the US. The absence of that driver is the story. The consensus talks about AI as a productivity salve, but the market implication may be that Canadian equities lack the earnings engine embedded in US equities.

### 10. Markets: CAD modestly stronger, rates stable-to-higher, Canadian equities mostly a valuation/sector-mix story

Consensus: CAD appreciates modestly, Canadian yields drift higher, and Canadian equities can do okay because of valuation, resources, banks, dividends, and diversification away from expensive US tech.

Evidence:

- BoC MPS median expected CAD at US$0.74 by end-2026 and US$0.75 by end-2027; WTI at US$75 then US$70.
- Spring Economic Update private economists expected CAD to average 73.7 US cents in 2026 and gradually rise to 75.8 cents by 2029.
- TD Wealth had a modest overweight to Canadian equities, citing more attractive valuations and exposure to energy/financials.
- BlackRock stayed pro-US equities and market-weight Canada, citing AI/tech leadership in the US and Canada trade risk.

Theme underneath: **Canada is a defensive/value/resource/bank allocation, not a growth allocation.**

Attackable angle: Fred is right that "Canada is cheaper" is not enough. The better equity question is corporate profit generation: does a larger public sector, weaker business investment, and absent AI landscape mechanically cap profit growth?

## Shared Themes Agreed Upon

These are the real themes that repeat across sources:

1. **Adjustment, not recession.** Canada is absorbing shocks but not escaping them.
2. **Slack does the disinflation work.** Excess supply is expected to keep inflation from becoming a regime problem.
3. **BoC is in a holding pattern.** The next meeting matters less than the 2027 path.
4. **Trade uncertainty has become background radiation.** It is important but no longer surprising.
5. **Public spending is part of the growth bridge.** Fiscal/infrastructure support is expected to offset private weakness.
6. **Private investment and productivity are the central structural problem.** Everyone says this; few turn it into a market call.
7. **AI is discussed as a possible solution, not proven as a Canadian earnings engine.**
8. **Labour weakness is real but contained.** Unemployment is elevated but expected to drift down.
9. **Consumption is slow and population-sensitive.** The denominator story matters.
10. **Canadian assets are framed as relative value / resource / bank exposure.** Less often as a profit-growth story.

## Red-Team Audit

Potential weaknesses in this memo:

1. This overweights public forecasts and underweights private conversations. True desk consensus may differ from published house views.
2. "Consensus" is not uniform: Scotia-style hawkishness, BMO's longer hold, TD/RBC slow-growth baselines, and asset-manager equity calls differ materially.
3. Some investment themes are asset-manager consensus, not economist consensus. They should not be mixed without labels.
4. The market has already repriced some BoC hike risk after the oil shock; "BoC hold through 2026" is a survey consensus, not necessarily live market pricing today.
5. The AI point needs more hard evidence before becoming a public claim. "Canada has no AI landscape" is a strong thesis, not yet proven by this source pass.

Referee conclusion: Safe to use this as a strategy memo and source map. Not safe to publish as a reader-facing note without tighter source cards and updated live market pricing.

## Sibley Creek Opportunity Set

Highest-value consensus targets:

1. **The investment rebound is assumed, not proven.**
   - Consensus assumes exports/business investment recover on a lower trajectory.
   - Sibley Creek can ask what private-sector mechanism actually delivers that recovery.

2. **The BoC debate is looking at the wrong horizon.**
   - Consensus obsesses over the next meeting.
   - Sibley Creek should frame the 2027 path and the market's mispriced regime.

3. **Retail sales is not consumption.**
   - Banks over-cover monthly retail sales.
   - Sibley Creek can show how household demand is actually constructed and what indicators matter.

4. **Canada's AI story is mostly absence.**
   - Consensus talks about AI productivity potential.
   - Sibley Creek can compare Canada's AI capex/equity/profit landscape to the US.

5. **Cheap equities are not a thesis.**
   - Canada-as-value is common.
   - Sibley Creek can reframe around profit share, private investment, sector composition, and public-sector drag.

6. **Trade uncertainty is now table stakes.**
   - Everyone knows it is a risk.
   - Sibley Creek should only write trade if it can identify a neglected mechanism, not just uncertainty.
