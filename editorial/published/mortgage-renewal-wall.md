## 2. Lede

> The mortgage renewal wall has peaked. The fixed-rate stock booked at
> 2020-21 contract rates -- the cohort that carried the bulk of the
> renewal-payment-shock distribution -- has now mostly rolled, and the
> mechanical pulse on household cash flows has begun to fade. Mortgage
> interest cost in the CPI tells the story most cleanly: at 0.28% Y/Y
> in March 2026, MIC's contribution to inflation is essentially
> extinguished, after running near double digits at the 2023-2024
> peak.
>
> The BoC held the overnight rate at 2.25% on April 29, 2026 -- the
> fourth consecutive hold since the October 29, 2025 cut. The floor on
> the renewal-rate spread is now stable; the residual transmission is
> about the height and persistence of that floor, not about how much
> further it falls.
>
> Peaked is not over, and what has peaked is narrower than the
> renewal-wall framing of 2024 implied. The BoC's Staff Analytical
> Paper 2026-12 (March 24, 2026) decomposes the historical drivers of
> Canadian mortgage arrears into a labour-market channel and a rate
> channel, and finds the labour market is the central force. The
> implied incremental arrears from a +300 bps tightening cycle is 10
> to 15 bps over two years; each 1 pp rise in the unemployment rate
> adds roughly 10 bps to the arrears rate within a year. The 2022-2023
> tightening has now mostly passed through the arrears series. The
> forward risk is no longer the renewal wall.
>
> This piece argues three things. First, the count of renewers facing
> a step-up in payment has peaked and the rate-channel pass-through is
> largely realized. Second, the household-side response has rotated
> from amortization extensions to discretionary-consumption
> compression -- a slower-burn channel that does not show up in any
> single monthly print. Third, the forward residual arrears risk has
> rotated with it: from the renewal-cohort tail, where the marginal
> incremental damage is small, to the labour-market channel, where the
> marginal lever is roughly 2 to 3 times larger per unit of shock.
> Section VII names what would change our mind.

## 3. What we got right; what we overstated

A short methodological note before Section I. The renewal-wall framing
that has dominated Canadian macro commentary since 2023 has done useful
work and has been overplayed; v3 marks both.

> **What we got right.** The mechanical renewal channel was real and
> meaningfully load-bearing in 2024-2025. The 5-year fixed cohort
> originated in 2020-2021 faced a step-up in contract rates of 250 to
> 400 bps at renewal, and the BoC's own modelling work in 2024 put the
> median 5-year fixed renewer in a 30 to 40% payment-increase band on
> that vintage. Mortgage interest cost ran near double digits in the
> CPI through the peak. Discretionary consumption compressed, the
> saving rate normalized downward, and the household-side response is
> visible in the macroeconomic aggregates. The pillar's framing of
> regional dispersion -- price-side concentrated in Toronto and
> Vancouver, credit-stress concentrated in the Prairies and Atlantic
> -- holds up against the February 2026 CBA vintage.
>
> **What we overstated.** The renewal wall was framed in 2024 as the
> dominant forward risk to Canadian household credit; we read the
> arrears channel through that lens. BoC SAP 2026-12 quantifies the
> rate channel and the labour channel as separate drivers and reports
> specific magnitudes: a full +300 bps tightening cycle is estimated
> to add 10 to 15 bps to the arrears rate over two years; a +1 pp rise
> in unemployment adds roughly 10 bps within a year. The 2022-2023
> tightening has now mostly transmitted -- the observed +8 bps move
> from the mid-2022 trough (0.14%) to the Q2 2025 reading (0.22%) sits
> inside that 10 to 15 bps range.
>
> The marginal residual from the rate channel is small; the marginal
> lever from the labour channel is comparable to the entire 2022-2023
> tightening on a per-pp basis. Canadian mortgage arrears are now
> mostly a labour-market story on the forward path. The renewal-wall
> mechanic -- still useful as a description of the backward-looking
> 2024-2025 stress -- is no longer the load-bearing frame for forward
> credit risk.

This is the reframe; the sections that follow respect it. The
renewal-wall mechanic is described in Sections I, II, and IV in its
proper backward-looking weight; the forward-looking arrears risk is
written through the labour channel in Section III and in the
falsification triggers in Section VII.

## 4. Section I -- The stock

What is outstanding, what is renewing when, and the size of the 2026
cohort.

> Federally-regulated banks hold C$1,952 billion of residential
> mortgage debt as of February 28, 2026, per OSFI's M4 Consolidated
> Balance Sheet (Total All Banks): C$433 billion insured, C$1,508
> billion uninsured, and C$11 billion reverse. This is the
> chartered-bank book that carries the renewal-wall residual. The
> all-lender total reported by CMHC for Q2 2025 is C$2.3 trillion; the
> chartered-bank book is therefore approximately 84% of the total
> Canadian residential mortgage stock, with the remaining 16% held by
> credit unions, MICs, and other non-federally-regulated lenders. Y/Y
> growth in the chartered-bank uninsured book is +1.5% (from C$1,484
> billion in February 2025); the insured book is -0.2% Y/Y. The
> Big-Six aggregate mortgage book sits at roughly C$1.76 trillion per
> Q1 FY2026 institutional filings, accounting for the bulk of the OSFI
> total.
>
> Within that stock, five-year fixed remains the dominant contract.
> BoC Staff Analytical Note 2025-1 (January 2025; data vintage
> September 2024) reports fixed-rate at roughly 80% of the stock and
> variable-rate at roughly 20%; five-year fixed alone is about 40% of
> the total. The cohorts that have framed the renewal-wall narrative
> since 2023 are those originated near the policy-rate floor of the
> 2020-21 pandemic window. The BoC does not publish the exact
> origination-rate band as a discrete figure, but the rolling-cohort
> averages are consistent with the canonical narrative shape of
> contract rates near the pandemic policy-rate floor.
>
> The renewal schedule from here is the variable that matters. Per BoC
> Staff Analytical Note 2025-21 (July 2025), 60% of all outstanding
> mortgages renew during 2025 or 2026 -- the load-bearing share. CMHC
> Residential Mortgage Industry Report Fall 2025 puts roughly 1.15
> million households renewing in 2026 specifically; CMHC's broader
> January 2026 framing places about 3.1 million mortgages (52% of
> total) renewing during 2026. The 2027 cohort is not separately
> published as a discrete number, but the rolling-cohort distribution
> implies approximately 20 to 30% of stock rolls in 2027. After 2027,
> the wave smooths.

## 5. Section II -- The stress

Payment-shock math.

> Payment shock at renewal is the cohort change in scheduled monthly
> mortgage payment when a contract resets from its original contract
> rate to the rate prevailing at renewal, holding amortization
> constant. BoC SAN 2025-21 frames this as a distribution rather than
> a single number, for the right reason: the median renewer's
> experience differs sharply from the tail's. The BoC's preferred
> denominator is the Mortgage Debt Service ratio -- mortgage-only debt
> service as a share of gross income -- not full DSR and not share of
> disposable income.
>
> The central estimate has been revised lower as rates have fallen.
> The BoC's 2024-vintage central case put the median 5-year fixed
> renewer in the 30 to 40% payment-increase band; that vintage is now
> outdated. The most recent BoC analytical work (Financial Stability
> Report 2025, May 2025; SAN 2025-21, July 2025) places the central
> estimate at 15 to 20% for 5-year fixed renewers in 2025 or 2026, and
> 6 to 10% across all renewing mortgages. The average monthly payment
> for 2025 renewers is roughly 10% above the pre-renewal level; for
> 2026 renewers, roughly 6%. Across payment increasers, median MDS
> rises 2.7 pp (from 15.3% to 18.0%); among payment decreasers --
> primarily short-term fixed and some variable-rate cohorts -- median
> MDS falls 1.1 pp (from 19.7% to 18.6%).
>
> The distribution is the load-bearing read. The top 10% of 2026
> renewers face a payment increase of more than 40%; the bottom 25%
> see at least a 7% decrease. Five-year fixed mortgages account for
> roughly 40% of all outstanding mortgages but about 75% of those
> facing payment increases at renewal. About one-third of all Canadian
> mortgage holders will see payment increases by end-2026; close to
> 25% see decreases. CIBC disclosed in its Q1 FY2026 earnings
> supplement that roughly 6% of its mortgage portfolio faces a payment
> shock of 40% or more in 2026 -- a clean read on the tail's
> concentration at one chartered bank.
>
> Three characteristics define the residual tail: original
> amortizations at or near 30 years (a feature of 2020-21 originations
> in expensive markets, not a fluke), uninsured loan-to-value ratios
> that leave little room to refinance into longer-amortization
> products without re-qualification under tighter B-20 stress-test
> conventions, and concentration in the Toronto and Vancouver CMAs
> where original nominal loan sizes were largest. CMHC RMIR Fall 2025
> reports the flow share of new uninsured mortgages at chartered banks
> with amortizations longer than 25 years remained above 60% in Q2
> 2025 for the fourth consecutive year.
>

## 6. Section III -- The response and the channel

Household side: extensions, consumption response, savings rate.
Arrears side: SAP 2026-12 decomposition; CBA February 2026 levels.

> The consumer-side response has rotated. In 2023-2024, the dominant
> margin of adjustment was structural: lenders extended amortizations,
> in some cases past the contractual maximum at the cost of a future
> re-amortization at re-qualification, to spread the renewal shock
> across a longer schedule. CMHC RMIR Fall 2025 shows the flow of new
> uninsured mortgages at chartered banks at amortizations longer than
> 25 years has held above 60% for four consecutive years. That margin
> has now largely been used: borrowers who could extend have, and the
> post-cut environment has reduced the incremental pressure to extend
> further.
>
> The current margin of adjustment is discretionary consumption.
> StatCan's quarterly expenditure cut shows household consumption at
> +0.4% Q/Q in Q4 2025 (+2.3% on the year), with the household saving
> rate at 4.4% in Q4 2025 and 4.9% for 2025 annual. Both numbers sit
> inside the post-COVID range and tell the same story: aggregate
> consumption is positive but soft, and the saving rate is normalizing
> downward from the 2023-2024 highs as households absorb the cash-flow
> imprint of higher debt service. Q1 2026 retail-trade prints confirm
> the shape: core retail ran +0.9% M/M in January and +0.6% M/M in
> February; March's advance estimate is +0.6% M/M. Positive but not
> strong. The renewal channel does not show up as a single month's
> collapse in retail sales; it shows up as a multi-quarter drag on the
> discretionary-services side, partly offsetting the mechanical fade
> of MIC in the inflation print.
>
> The forward arrears risk does not run primarily through the renewal
> channel. BoC Staff Analytical Paper 2026-12 (March 24, 2026) -- the
> renamed continuation of the staff-analytical series starting with
> the 2026 vintage -- decomposes the historical drivers of Canadian
> mortgage arrears using monthly data from 1980 through 2017 and a
> panel of provincial regressions from 1990 through 2019. The paper's
> section heading is direct: "The labour market is the key driver of
> arrears." The decomposition assigns the central role to the
> labour-market channel, with mortgage interest rates as the
> cyclically important second-order force; house price growth, core
> inflation, and the output gap are less significant contributors.
>
> The magnitudes are specific. From the paper's SVAR results: a +1 pp
> increase in the unemployment rate raises the arrears rate by
> approximately 10 bps after one year. A +100 bps increase in mortgage
> interest rates raises the arrears rate by 3 to 5 bps after two
> years. The implied incremental arrears from a +300 bps tightening
> cycle -- roughly the magnitude of the 2022-2023 BoC tightening -- is
> 10 to 15 bps. The arrears rate ran at 0.2% before the pandemic and
> reached 0.5% at the peak of the global financial crisis; SAP 2026-12
> frames forward stress as potentially surpassing the GFC peak but not
> the 1990s peaks, conditional on the labour market.
>
> Two implications follow. First, the rate-channel pass-through from
> the 2022-2023 tightening is largely realized. The observed move in
> the CBA national arrears series from the 0.14% mid-2022 trough to
> the 0.22% Q2 2025 reading (+8 bps) sits inside the SAP 2026-12
> model's projected 10 to 15 bps range -- meaning the bulk of the
> renewal-wall arrears effect is already in the printed series. The
> residual transmission from the rate cycle is small. Second, each
> percentage point of incremental unemployment carries roughly the
> same arrears impact (10 bps) as the entirety of the 2022-2023
> rate-tightening cycle. The forward risk to Canadian household credit
> is now primarily a labour-market story, not a renewal-wall story.
> The two channels move independently.
>
> The geography of arrears confirms the framing. CBA's chartered-bank
> monthly arrears series for February 2026 reads 0.28% nationally
> (13,749 mortgages in arrears out of 4,937,235 covered). The
> provincial breakdown:
>
> | Province | Feb 2026 | Comment |
> |---|---|---|
> | Saskatchewan | 0.53% | Highest; energy-cycle and labour-driven |
> | Manitoba | 0.36% | Second-highest; provincial-labour driven |
> | Atlantic | 0.31% | Third; labour and demographic mix |
> | Ontario | 0.30% | Now near Atlantic; +4 bps Jan->Feb 2026 |
> | National | 0.28% | All chartered-bank book |
> | Alberta | 0.27% | Below national; below historical Prairie cluster |
> | British Columbia | 0.25% | +4 bps Jan->Feb 2026 |
> | Quebec | 0.19% | Lowest |
>
> Two refinements vs v2. Ontario at 0.30% in February 2026 has nearly
> converged with Atlantic Canada in levels -- a 1 bp gap, after a +4
> bps Jan-to-Feb move. The price-side regional dispersion (Toronto and
> Vancouver leading the national MLS HPI turn) and the credit-stress
> dispersion (Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Atlantic leading by level;
> Ontario and BC leading by growth rate) remain different cuts.
> Ontario's growth-rate lead is now producing a level convergence. The
> provincial-arrears geography remains energy-cycle and
> provincial-labour-market driven, not renewal-cohort driven --
> consistent with the SAP 2026-12 decomposition.
>
> Big-Six bank provisions reflect the same regional tilt. Q1 FY2026
> (November 1, 2025 through January 31, 2026) institutional filings
> show combined writeoffs of C$38 million on the C$1.76 trillion
> combined mortgage portfolio; trailing four quarters, C$168 million
> in writeoffs, equivalent to 0.01% of mortgage holdings. RBC's own
> disclosure cites an "uptick in impaired loans driven by residential
> mortgages, particularly in Ontario"; CIBC's Q1 FY2026 supplement
> places roughly 6% of its mortgage portfolio in the 40%-or-more
> payment-shock band in 2026. The build is real but concentrated; the
> system-level loss read remains near a cyclical floor.

## 7. Section IV -- The tail

What is still to come: 2026H2 and 2027 cohorts.

> The tail of the renewal wall is structurally smaller than its peak
> and mechanically slower to clear, and the forward arrears
> implications are smaller again than the count of renewers would
> imply. The BoC has cut 275 bps from the mid-2024 peak (5.0% to 2.25%), and
> the overnight rate at 2.25% has now been stable for four consecutive
> meetings. The marginal incremental relief from further cuts is
> largely gone. The 2027 cohort rolls into a rate environment the
> market expects to be broadly stable from 2026 levels; the post-2021
> origination cohorts roll into rates closer to what they were booked
> at, because the 2022 originations were already partly repriced into
> the BoC's hiking cycle. The remaining renewal-rate spread for the
> 2026H2 and 2027 cohorts combined is materially narrower than the
> spread the 2024-2025 cohorts faced.
>
> The arrears implication of this residual is modest. Per SAP
> 2026-12's magnitudes, the entire +300 bps 2022-2023 tightening cycle
> is estimated to add 10 to 15 bps to the arrears rate over two years;
> the observed +8 bps move from the 2022 trough to the Q2 2025 reading
> is consistent with the bulk of that pass-through having occurred.
> The residual incremental rate-channel arrears contribution from the
> 2026H2 and 2027 cohorts -- which face a renewal-rate spread perhaps
> a quarter of the spread the 2023-2024 cohorts faced -- is therefore
> small in absolute terms. The renewal channel is not the binding
> forward arrears risk. What matters more for the next leg is whether
> the labour market turns.
>
> The residual transmission is now about the height and persistence of
> the floor, not about how much further it falls. The BoC's April 29,
> 2026 decision held at 2.25% (Bank Rate 2.5%, deposit rate 2.20%);
> the April MPR forward guidance is conditional on the CUSMA trade
> agreement, the Middle East conflict, and the trajectory of US
> tariffs and energy prices. The MPR projects inflation rising in the
> near term and easing toward 2% in early 2027, with GDP growth of
> 1.2% in 2026, 1.6% in 2027, and 1.7% in 2028. The MPR does not give
> an explicit terminal-rate forecast. A pause-and-hold path from 2.25%
> delivers a different distribution of renewal-rate spreads than a
> continued-cut path, but the convex distribution of payment-shock
> outcomes around the floor matters less for forward arrears than the
> unemployment path does.
>
> The variable-rate cohort is not the wildcard it was once framed to
> be. BoC SAN 2025-1 and SAN 2025-21 place the variable-rate share at
> roughly 20% of stock; among variable-rate borrowers with
> fixed-payment contracts, approximately 12% were in negative
> amortization as of September 2024 -- equivalent to about 2% of all
> mortgages. The pre-2022 variable-rate fixed-payment cohort behaved
> more responsibly than the contractual mechanics predicted: 80% of
> those borrowers made extra principal payments (averaging three times
> the required amount), and the realized negative-amortization share
> is approximately 5% versus an expected 25% from contract terms
> alone.
>
> The regional dispersion widens through the tail in price terms, not
> in arrears levels. CREA MLS HPI shows national benchmark prices down
> 4.7% Y/Y in March 2026; Toronto's benchmark stood at C$944,100 in
> April 2026, down 6.6% Y/Y; Vancouver was at C$1,101,700 in March
> 2026, down 6.7% Y/Y. Toronto and Vancouver are leading the national
> turn -- more sharply than the national-aggregate read suggests.
> Negative price momentum interacts with renewal stress through the
> uninsured-LTV channel: borrowers whose original LTV was tight have
> less room to refinance into a different product, and the set of
> available products narrows further when the property's
> mark-to-market value has fallen. The arrears-rate response to price
> weakness remains lagged. CMHC's Toronto projection to 0.34% by Q4
> 2026 is the cleanest published path.
>

## 8. Section V -- What would change our view

> Three triggers fire against this view; the falsification thresholds
> have been re-cast around the labour channel as the larger forward
> lever.
>
> **The wall peaks earlier than this piece claims if**: CMHC's
> mortgage arrears rate (90+ days) stalls or rolls over within the
> next two RMIR releases (the Spring 2026 vintage is not yet released
> as of 2026-05-11; convention is semi-annual); CBA's chartered-bank
> monthly arrears falls below 0.25% for three consecutive months from
> the current 0.28% reading; and Big-Six writeoffs on residential
> mortgages trail below the current C$168 million four-quarter
> baseline in the Q2 FY2026 earnings cycle (mid-2026). The combination
> would say the 2024-2025 stress has begun to dissipate faster than
> the cohort-mechanics model predicts; the tail is shorter and the
> labour market has not yet turned hard enough to keep the series
> elevated.
>
> **The wall extends past 2027 if**: Canadian unemployment moves above
> 7.5% on a sustained basis (two consecutive quarters), or the LFS
> prints back-to-back monthly increases of 0.2 pp or more in the
> unemployment rate. This is the load-bearing trigger after the SAP
> 2026-12 reframe: per the paper's SVAR estimates, a +1 pp rise in
> unemployment adds roughly 10 bps to the arrears rate within a year
> -- comparable in magnitude to the entirety of the 2022-2023
> rate-channel pass-through. The April 2026 LFS print (released May 9,
> 2026) has unemployment at 6.9%; a 50 bps move above that, sustained,
> would put the arrears trajectory on a path materially worse than the
> renewal-channel modelling alone would predict. CMHC arrears
> continuing to rise through 2026H2 and into 2027 without inflecting
> -- with the Toronto sub-aggregate moving above 0.40% (above CMHC's
> own Q4 2026 projection of 0.34%) -- would be a supporting indicator.
> A 5-year GoC yield above 4.0% would re-widen the renewal-rate
> spread, but the marginal arrears impact of that re-widening is small
> relative to the labour channel.
>
> **We are wrong about the regional-credit framing if**: the
> growth-rate convergence between Ontario and the historical
> high-arrears provinces continues such that Ontario crosses Manitoba
> in levels (currently 0.30% vs 0.36%), or if Alberta's CBA arrears
> rate moves materially above the national average -- say, above 0.35%
> on the monthly series, given the current Alberta reading of 0.27%
> sits modestly below national. The current geography says
> credit-stress levels are Prairie- and Atlantic-led with Ontario
> closing fast; a clean Alberta breakout would say the energy-cycle
> channel has become a dominant story independently of the renewal
> wave.
>
> The cheapest test that would resolve the Section III reframe is the
> next LFS release (June 6, 2026 for May 2026 data). Labour-market
> deterioration shows up in the LFS first, in the CBA arrears series
> with roughly a one-year lag per SAP 2026-12. The second-cheapest
> test is the next CMHC RMIR vintage (Spring 2026, release date not
> yet announced), which will mark the regional arrears geography
> against the February 2026 CBA readings. If the next LFS holds
> unemployment at or below 6.9% and the Spring 2026 RMIR shows the
> regional arrears geography stable, the renewal-wall residual is the
> backward-looking story this piece claims it is. If the LFS prints a
> 50 bps deterioration, the forward read flips to the labour channel
> even faster than this piece anticipates.
