## 2. Lede

In March 2026, 66.1% of Canada's merchandise exports went to the
United States. In calendar 2024, the figure averaged 76.3%. The
share has fallen roughly ten percentage points in fourteen months.

It is tempting to read that as a collapse. It is not. Total
Canadian merchandise exports hit a record CAD 68.8 billion in
March 2026, well above the pre-tariff baseline near CAD 61 billion.
The trade balance with the United States widened in Canada's
favour to CAD 7.1 billion. The overall trade balance flipped from
a CAD 2.4 billion deficit a year earlier to a CAD 1.8 billion
surplus. Exports did not shrink. Their geography moved.

That movement is the largest peacetime reorientation of Canadian
trade since the late-1980s free-trade negotiations. Most of the
public argument is still pointed at the July 1 USMCA joint review
as if it were the decisive event. The decisive event is already
behind us. The question now is whether the reorientation
consolidates or partially unwinds.

This piece argues that the reorientation is the load-bearing fact
of Canadian macro for 2026, and that the durable policy threat
running underneath it is not the IEEPA tariff regime imposed in
February 2025 -- it is the Section 232 stack on steel, aluminum,
copper, autos, and softwood lumber. That stack is sector-targeted,
legally distinct from IEEPA, and operating today.

## 3. What actually escalated

The 2025 tariff record is busy enough that it is worth laying out
in one place.

Two tracks ran in parallel through 2025. The first is the IEEPA
track. On 2025-02-01, the United States invoked the International
Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose 25% tariffs on non-CUSMA-
compliant Canadian goods and 10% on energy and potash, effective
2025-02-04. On 2025-08-01, the non-compliant rate rose to 35%.
Approximately 98% of Canada-US tariff lines and 99.9% of bilateral
trade qualify as CUSMA-compliant when the documentation is
prepared, so the IEEPA rates bind on a narrower base than the
headlines suggested.

The second track is Section 232 -- the national-security tariff
authority -- and it is the one that matters.

- Steel: raised from 25% to 50% on 2025-06-04.
- Aluminum: raised from 25% to 50% on 2025-06-04.
- Copper: 50% on core articles and 25% on derivatives, added
  2026-04-06 under a 2026-04-02 proclamation.
- Autos and auto parts: 25%.
- Softwood lumber: 10% Section 232 stacked on top of the existing
  countervailing and anti-dumping duties, effective October 2025.

A de minimis carve-out arrived in April 2026: goods containing
15% or less subject metal content are exempt. A relief path also
opened that month allowing Canadian and Mexican producers to
qualify for reduced Section 232 rates by committing to build
primary capacity inside the United States. Both are narrow.

![What's actually in force](/charts/trade-tariffs/tariff-matrix.svg)
*Caption: Tariff regimes by sector and authority, current as of
2026-05-11. Rows: energy, autos, steel, aluminum, copper, softwood
lumber, dairy, other. Columns: IEEPA non-compliant rate, Section
232 rate, AD/CVD. Source: US Federal Register, Congressional
Research Service IN12519, US Commerce Department proclamations.*

The Section 232 stack is the durable threat for three reasons.
It rests on a different statutory authority than IEEPA. It is
sector-targeted rather than country-targeted, which makes it
politically resilient -- it has constituencies inside the United
States, not just opponents. And it operates today, with rates
already at 50% on the metals lines and a stacked 45% effective
burden on softwood lumber.

## 4. The softwood arithmetic

Softwood lumber is the sharpest illustration of how the two tariff
systems stack.

The anti-dumping and countervailing duty rate on Canadian softwood
sat at a combined 35.16% before April 2026. The Section 232 rate
on lumber adds another 10%. The effective burden today is
approximately 45%.

A preliminary Commerce Department review published on 2026-04-14
proposes cutting the AD/CVD components to 24.83% (anti-dumping of
10.66% plus countervailing of 14.17%), pending final determinations
in August and October 2026. If those preliminary cuts hold, the
effective burden falls to about 34.83% -- still high, still
binding, and still entirely separate from anything USMCA can fix
on July 1.

![Lumber stack](/charts/trade-tariffs/lumber-stack.svg)
*Caption: Softwood lumber effective tariff stack -- AD plus CVD
plus Section 232 -- before and after the April 2026 preliminary
review. Source: US Federal Register notice 2026-07154 (2026-04-14),
US Commerce Department, Congressional Research Service R48781.*

That is the texture worth holding onto. Even a "win" in the
softwood review leaves British Columbia mills facing a tariff
burden equivalent to roughly a third of cargo value. The metals
and auto tracks are at higher rates than the post-cut lumber rate.

## 5. The reorientation, decomposed

Back to the headline fact. The US export share moved from 76.3%
to 66.1% in fourteen months. What is inside that move?

Three forces are mixing in roughly unknown proportions. The first
is genuine demand diversion -- Canadian firms shipping to Europe,
Asia, and other non-US destinations in response to tariff
exposure. The second is the unwind of front-loading distortions
that pulled US-bound shipments into early 2025 ahead of expected
tariff escalations, now reversing in Q1 2026. The third is
currency: USD/CAD dynamics over the period affected the CAD
denomination of US-bound flows.

A clean decomposition requires commodity-by-destination detail
that the project does not yet have in raw form. Statistics Canada
Table 12-10-0011 carries the bilateral commodity breakdown
needed; we have not pulled it. The aggregate evidence is enough,
though, to make the structural point.

The aggregate point: total exports rose to a record while US
share fell. That combination rules out the simplest reading --
that US tariffs caused a Canadian export contraction. They did
not. They caused a redirection.

![Total exports rose, US share fell](/charts/trade-tariffs/exports-dual-axis.svg)
*Caption: Total Canadian merchandise exports (bars, left axis,
CAD billions) and US share (line, right axis, percent), monthly,
January 2023 to March 2026. Annotations mark IEEPA imposition
(February 2025) and Section 232 escalations (June 2025). Source:
Statistics Canada international merchandise trade, customs basis,
seasonally adjusted.*

What we cannot yet say is how much of the diverted volume is
"sticky" -- that is, how much of it represents new commercial
relationships that will persist if US tariffs ease versus
opportunistic spot trade that reverses on the first sign of
relief. That is the central uncertainty in any 2026-H2 forecast
of where US share lands. Our working assumption is partial
unwind: from 66-70% in Q1 2026 toward 70-72% by year-end if no
new escalation arrives. We flag this as inference, not data.

## 6. The Bank of Canada's calibration -- and what it implicitly assumes

The most-cited macro pass-through estimate is the Bank of Canada's
January 2025 scenario. It is worth being precise about what it
says and what it does not.

The scenario specification: a permanent 25% US tariff on all
imports including Canada, plus 25% Canadian retaliation. The
benchmark output: Canadian GDP growth in Year 1 is roughly 2.5
percentage points lower than the counterfactual path. A 2%
baseline becomes -0.5%; a 0.5% Year 2 follows. Tariff pass-through
to consumer prices is initially low and builds toward full pass-
through over three years.

That scenario was calibrated to a broad, symmetric, country-level
shock. What we actually got in 2025 and early 2026 is a stack of
sectoral Section 232 tariffs plus a narrow IEEPA regime that
binds on a small share of trade. The realized shape is
asymmetric -- concentrated in metals, autos, and lumber -- not
the broad shock the scenario modelled.

That matters for two reasons. First, the realized GDP drag should
come in below the 2.5-percentage-point scenario, because the
base is narrower. The Bank's July 2025 update noted that observed
tariff effects on inflation had been limited to date, with core
inflation rising from roughly 2% in late 2024 to about 2.5% by
June 2025, mostly in non-energy goods. That is consistent with a
narrower-than-modelled pass-through.

Second, the political-economy distribution is the opposite of
diffuse. A 50% aluminum tariff that adds a small share of a point
to national GDP can take a significant chunk out of the Quebec
aluminum corridor. A 25% auto tariff aims at Ontario. The 45%
lumber stack aims at British Columbia. The national-aggregate
framing understates the provincial concentration, and that
concentration is where the policy pressure on the federal
response will come from.

![Scenario versus realized](/charts/trade-tariffs/boc-scenario-vs-realized.svg)
*Caption: Bank of Canada January 2025 Monetary Policy Report
tariff scenario (benchmark path) versus realized Canadian GDP
growth, quarterly, 2025-Q1 through 2026-Q1. Source: Bank of
Canada MPR 2025-01-29 In-Focus 1; Statistics Canada Table
36-10-0104.*

On the Bank's reaction function: Governor Macklem's February 2025
speech "Tariffs, structural change and monetary policy" set the
framework. Tariffs are a supply-side shock. The Bank looks through
first-round price-level effects and responds only if inflation
expectations de-anchor or wage-price dynamics emerge. The April
2025 fixed announcement held the policy rate at 2.75% with
explicit reference to tariff uncertainty constraining forward
guidance. The Bank has since presented scenario analysis rather
than point forecasts in its Monetary Policy Reports -- the first
time it has done so since COVID -- because the tariff path is
unknowable.

## 7. USMCA, July 1, and what the review actually is

The market narrative treats the USMCA joint review on 2026-07-01
as a renegotiation flashpoint. It is closer to the opposite.

Article 34.7 of USMCA sets a sixth-anniversary joint review on
2026-07-01. Each of the three parties must confirm in writing
whether to extend the agreement's sixteen-year term. If all three
confirm, the agreement runs to 2042. If any one party objects, the
agreement enters annual review mode with a sunset trajectory to
2036. Recommendations for substantive changes must be submitted at
least one month before the review, meaning the practical deadline
is 2026-06-01.

The mechanism's default is extension. Substantive renegotiation
requires unanimity; a single party declining to confirm does not
automatically reopen the text -- it triggers a slower review
process. The structural bias is toward continuation.

That is not the same thing as saying renegotiation will not
happen. The provisions widely flagged as in play include auto
rules of origin (regional value content thresholds and labour-
value content), Chapter 31 dispute settlement, dairy tariff-rate
quotas, digital trade carve-outs, and anti-circumvention. The
review is a real policy venue. But the binary "USMCA collapses on
July 1" framing common in market commentary is structurally
unlikely.

![USMCA review calendar](/charts/trade-tariffs/usmca-timeline.svg)
*Caption: USMCA joint review calendar -- September 2025 USTR
consultations open, 2026-06-01 recommendations deadline,
2026-07-01 joint review trigger, confirmation deadline. Source:
Congressional Research Service R48787 and IF10997; US Federal
Register notice 2025-18010 (2025-09-17).*

The deep-dive implication: the July 1 date is not the decisive
event in the Canadian tariff story. The Section 232 stack is the
decisive policy, and it sits outside USMCA's scope entirely.
USMCA can be renewed cleanly on July 1 and the steel, aluminum,
copper, auto, and lumber tariffs will still be in force on July 2.

## 8. What would change our mind

The argument here rests on three load-bearing facts: a ten-point
US share drop in fourteen months, a Section 232 stack operating
on five sectors today, and a USMCA review whose default is
extension. The thesis is that the structural reorientation has
already happened, that Section 232 is the durable channel running
underneath it, and that July 1 is not the binary event the
narrative implies.

Three things would falsify this reading.

The first is a sharp reversal of the US share decline. If Q2 and
Q3 2026 prints take the US share back above 75% with no
corresponding tariff de-escalation, the "structural reorientation"
framing is wrong and we were looking at front-loading distortions
all along. We will know by the August and October 2026 trade
prints.

The second is a Section 232 climb-down -- meaningful rollback of
the 50% metals tariffs or the 25% auto rate through the new
investment-based relief path or through bilateral negotiation. If
the durable channel softens materially, the macro pass-through
mostly evaporates and the BoC scenario calibration becomes a
ceiling rather than a benchmark.

The third is a USMCA fracture on July 1 -- one of the three
parties declining to confirm extension. Default mechanics make
that unlikely but not impossible. A fracture would shift average
Canada-US trade toward most-favoured-nation tariff schedules over
time, with the highest peaks in sectors USMCA had been quietly
protecting. We do not yet have the trade-weighted MFN average
needed to size that shock, and we flag it.

The single print we are watching hardest is the May and June 2026
trade releases, both for the share trajectory and for any
commodity-level evidence on where the diverted volumes are
actually going. The single date we are watching hardest is
2026-07-01.
