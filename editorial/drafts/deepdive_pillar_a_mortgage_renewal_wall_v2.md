## 2. Lede

Three paragraphs. State the question, state the answer, name the
channel that still matters.

> The mortgage renewal wall has peaked. The fixed-rate stock booked
> at 2020-21 contract rates -- the cohort that has carried the bulk
> of the renewal-payment-shock distribution -- has now mostly rolled,
> and the mechanical pulse on household cash flows has begun to fade.
> Mortgage interest cost in the CPI tells the story most cleanly: at
> 0.28% Y/Y in March 2026, MIC's contribution to inflation is
> essentially extinguished, after running near double digits at the
> 2023-2024 peak. The Bank of Canada held the overnight rate at 2.25%
> on April 29, 2026 -- the fourth consecutive hold since the October
> 29, 2025 cut. The floor on the renewal-rate spread is now stable;
> the residual transmission is about the height and persistence of
> that floor, not about how much further it falls.
>
> But peaked is not over. 60% of all outstanding mortgages renew in
> 2025 or 2026; roughly 1.15 million households renew in 2026 alone,
> and CMHC's framing of the broader pool puts about 3.1 million
> mortgages -- 52% of the total -- renewing during 2026. The 2026
> cohort rolls at average contract rates that are still meaningfully
> above what they replaced, and the 2027 cohort renews into a rate
> environment the market expects to be broadly stable from here. The
> shape of the distribution has changed more than the size: payment
> shocks are now concentrated in a smaller tail with longer original
> amortizations and weaker equity buffers. Regional dispersion --
> price-side in Toronto and Vancouver, credit-stress-side in the
> Prairies and Atlantic -- is wider than the national aggregate
> suggests. Arrears are still low by Canadian historical standards
> and still moving in the wrong direction.
>
> The piece that follows argues three things. First, that the *count*
> of renewers facing a step-up in payment has peaked, but the
> *severity* in the remaining tail is the variable to watch through
> 2027. Second, that the consumer-side response has shifted from
> "spread the shock through extended amortizations" to "absorb the
> shock through lower discretionary consumption" -- a slower-burn
> channel that does not show up cleanly in any single monthly print.
> Third, that the bank-side residual is two regional stories at once:
> price-side dispersion concentrated in Toronto and Vancouver, and
> credit-stress dispersion concentrated in Saskatchewan, Manitoba,
> and Atlantic Canada by level, with Ontario and BC leading in growth
> rate of stress. We name what would change our mind in Section VII.

## 3. Section I -- The stock

What is outstanding, what is renewing when, and the size of the
2026 cohort.

> Canada's residential mortgage stock stood at roughly C$2.3 trillion
> as of August 2025, growing 4.8% Y/Y, per CMHC's Residential
> Mortgage Industry Report Fall 2025. The chartered-bank share of
> that stock is approximately 80%; credit unions, private lenders,
> and MICs make up the remainder. Of the chartered-bank portion, the
> Big-Six aggregate mortgage book sits at roughly C$1.76 trillion as
> of Q1 FY2026 disclosures.
>
> Within that stock, five-year fixed remains the dominant contract.
> The BoC's Staff Analytical Note 2025-1 (January 2025, data vintage
> September 2024) reports fixed-rate at roughly 80% of the stock and
> variable-rate at roughly 20%; five-year fixed alone is about 40%
> of the total. The cohorts that have framed the renewal-wall
> narrative since 2023 are those originated near the policy-rate
> floor of the 2020-21 pandemic window. The BoC does not publish the
> exact origination-rate band as a discrete figure, but the rolling-
> cohort averages are consistent with the canonical narrative shape
> of contract rates near the pandemic policy-rate floor.
>
> The renewal schedule from here is the variable that matters. Per
> BoC Staff Analytical Note 2025-21 (July 2025), 60% of all
> outstanding mortgages renew during 2025 or 2026 -- the load-bearing
> share. CMHC's Residential Mortgage Industry Report Fall 2025 puts
> roughly 1.15 million households renewing in 2026 specifically;
> CMHC's broader January 2026 framing places about 3.1 million
> mortgages (52% of total) renewing during the 2026 calendar year.
> The 2027 cohort is not separately published as a discrete number,
> but the rolling-cohort distribution implies approximately 20 to
> 30% of stock rolls in 2027. After 2027, the wave smooths -- the
> post-2021 origination cohorts roll into rates closer to where they
> were booked, and the renewal-spread channel narrows mechanically.

## 4. Section II -- The stress

Payment shock math.

> Payment shock at renewal is the cohort change in scheduled monthly
> mortgage payment when a contract resets from its original contract
> rate to the rate prevailing at renewal, holding amortization
> constant. BoC SAN 2025-21 frames this as a distribution rather
> than a single number, for the right reason: the median renewer's
> experience differs sharply from the tail's. The BoC's preferred
> denominator is the Mortgage Debt Service ratio -- mortgage-only
> debt service as a share of gross income -- not full DSR and not
> share of disposable income.
>
> The central estimate has been revised lower as rates have fallen.
> The BoC's 2024-vintage central case put the median 5-year fixed
> renewer in the 30 to 40% payment-increase band; that vintage is
> now outdated. The most recent BoC analytical work (Financial
> Stability Report 2025, May 2025; SAN 2025-21, July 2025) places
> the central estimate at 15 to 20% for 5-year fixed renewers in
> 2025 or 2026, and 6 to 10% across all renewing mortgages. The
> average monthly payment for 2025 renewers is roughly 10% above
> the pre-renewal level; for 2026 renewers, roughly 6%. Across
> payment increasers, median MDS rises 2.7 percentage points (from
> 15.3% to 18.0%); among payment decreasers -- primarily short-term
> fixed and some variable-rate cohorts -- median MDS falls 1.1
> percentage points (from 19.7% to 18.6%).
>
> The distribution is the load-bearing read. The top 10% of 2026
> renewers face a payment increase of more than 40%; the bottom 25%
> see at least a 7% decrease. Five-year fixed mortgages account for
> roughly 40% of all outstanding mortgages, but about 75% of those
> facing payment increases at renewal. About one-third of all
> Canadian mortgage holders will see payment increases by end-2026;
> close to 25% see decreases. CIBC disclosed in its Q1 FY2026
> earnings supplement that roughly 6% of its mortgage portfolio
> faces a payment shock of 40% or more in 2026 -- a clean read on
> the tail's concentration at one chartered bank.
>
> Three characteristics define the residual tail: original
> amortizations at or near 30 years (a feature of 2020-21
> originations in expensive markets, not a fluke), uninsured loan-
> to-value ratios that leave little room to refinance into longer
> amortization products without re-qualification under tighter B-20
> stress-test conventions, and concentration in the Toronto and
> Vancouver CMAs where original nominal loan sizes were largest.
> CMHC's Residential Mortgage Industry Report Fall 2025 reports the
> *flow* share of new uninsured mortgages at chartered banks with
> amortizations longer than 25 years remained above 60% in Q2 2025
> for the fourth consecutive year; the stock-share read at the
> equivalent threshold remains a TK pending an OSFI M4 direct pull.
>

## 5. Section III -- The response

Household side: extensions, consumption response, savings rate.
Bank side: arrears geography, PCL, exposure.

> The consumer-side response has rotated. In 2023-2024, the
> dominant margin of adjustment was structural: lenders extended
> amortizations, in some cases past the contractual maximum at the
> cost of a future re-amortization at re-qualification, to spread
> the renewal shock across a longer schedule. CMHC RMIR Fall 2025
> shows the flow of new uninsured mortgages at chartered banks at
> amortizations longer than 25 years has held above 60% for four
> consecutive years. That margin has now largely been used:
> borrowers who could extend have, and the post-cut environment
> has reduced the incremental pressure to extend further.
>
> The current margin of adjustment is discretionary consumption.
> StatCan's quarterly expenditure cut shows household consumption
> at 0.4% Q/Q in Q4 2025 (2.3% on the year), with the household
> saving rate at 4.4% in Q4 2025 and 4.9% for 2025 annual. Both
> numbers sit inside the post-COVID range and tell the same story:
> aggregate consumption is positive but soft, and the saving rate
> is normalizing downward from the 2023-2024 highs as households
> absorb the cash-flow imprint of higher debt service. Q1 2026
> retail-trade prints confirm the shape: core retail (ex-auto,
> ex-gas) ran +0.9% M/M in January and +0.6% M/M in February;
> March's advance estimate is +0.6% M/M. Positive but not strong.
> The renewal channel does not show up as a single month's
> collapse in retail sales; it shows up as a multi-quarter drag
> on the discretionary-services side, partly offsetting the
> mechanical fade of MIC in the inflation print.
>
> The bank-side residual is two regional stories at once, and they
> are not the same geography. CMHC's national arrears rate (90+
> days past due) was 0.22% in Q2 2025, up from 0.19% a year
> earlier. Ontario printed 0.23% -- the first time above the
> national average since at least 2012 -- with Toronto at 0.24%
> (up from 0.15% Y/Y) and BC at 0.19%. CMHC projects Toronto
> arrears to 0.34% by Q4 2026.
>
> By level, however, the highest-arrears geographies are not
> Toronto or Vancouver. CBA's chartered-bank monthly arrears
> series (national 0.28% in February 2026, up 5 bps Y/Y; January
> 2026 provincial breakdown) shows Saskatchewan at 0.50%,
> Manitoba at 0.35%, and Atlantic Canada at 0.29% -- well above
> Ontario at 0.26%, Alberta at 0.25%, BC at 0.21%, and Quebec at
> 0.19%. The provincial-arrears geography is energy-cycle and
> provincial-labour-market driven, not renewal-cohort driven.
> Ontario and Toronto lead the *growth rate* of arrears
> (Toronto +60% Y/Y, Ontario +44% Y/Y), but Saskatchewan and
> Manitoba lead the *levels*. The price-side regional dispersion
> (Toronto and Vancouver) and the credit-stress-side dispersion
> (Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Atlantic in levels; Ontario and BC in
> growth rate) are different cuts. The piece reads them as
> different cuts. [TK pending researcher wave 5 direct parse of
> BoC SAN 2026-12 ("Examining the macro drivers of mortgage
> arrears in Canada", March 2026) and the CBA February 2026
> arrears PDF.]
>
> Big-Six bank provisions reflect the same regional tilt. Q1
> FY2026 (period November 1, 2025 through January 31, 2026)
> earnings supplements show combined writeoffs of C$38 million
> on the C$1.76 trillion combined mortgage portfolio; trailing
> four quarters, C$168 million in writeoffs, equivalent to 0.01%
> of mortgage holdings. RBC's own disclosure cites an "uptick in
> impaired loans driven by residential mortgages, particularly
> in Ontario"; CIBC's Q1 FY2026 supplement places roughly 6% of
> its mortgage portfolio in the 40%-or-more payment-shock band
> in 2026. The build is real but concentrated; the system-level
> loss read remains near a cyclical floor.
>

## 6. Section IV -- The tail

What is still to come: 2026H2 and 2027 cohorts.

> The tail of the renewal wall is structurally smaller than its
> peak but mechanically slower to clear. The 2026 cohort rolls at
> rates that are still above what they replaced, but the gap has
> narrowed: the BoC has already cut roughly 250 bps from the
> mid-2024 peak, and the overnight rate at 2.25% has now been
> stable for four consecutive meetings. The marginal incremental
> relief from further cuts is largely gone. The 2027 cohort rolls
> into a rate environment the market expects to be broadly stable
> from 2026 levels; the post-2021 origination cohorts roll into
> rates closer to what they were booked at, because the 2022
> originations were already partly repriced into the BoC's hiking
> cycle. The remaining renewal-rate spread for the 2026H2 and
> 2027 cohorts combined is materially narrower than the spread
> the 2024-2025 cohorts faced.
>
> The residual transmission is now about the height and
> persistence of the floor, not about how much further it falls.
> The BoC's April 29, 2026 decision held at 2.25% (Bank Rate
> 2.5%, deposit rate 2.20%); the April MPR forward guidance is
> conditional on the CUSMA trade agreement, the Middle East
> conflict, and the trajectory of US tariffs and energy prices.
> The MPR projects inflation rising in the near term and easing
> toward 2% in early 2027, with GDP growth of 1.2% in 2026, 1.6%
> in 2027, and 1.7% in 2028. There is no explicit terminal-rate
> forecast. A pause-and-hold path from 2.25% delivers a different
> distribution of renewal-rate spreads than a continued-cut path,
> and the renewal-payment-shock math is convex in the renewal-
> rate spread.
>
> The variable-rate cohort is not the wildcard it was once framed
> to be. BoC SAN 2025-1 and SAN 2025-21 place the variable-rate
> share at roughly 20% of stock; among variable-rate borrowers
> with fixed-payment contracts, approximately 12% were in negative
> amortization as of September 2024 -- equivalent to about 2% of
> all mortgages. The pre-2022 variable-rate fixed-payment cohort
> behaved more responsibly than the contractual mechanics
> predicted: 80% of those borrowers made extra principal payments
> (averaging three times the required amount), and the realized
> negative-amortization share is approximately 5% versus an
> expected 25% from contract terms alone. The variable-rate
> cohort is smaller and better-buffered than the 2023-vintage
> "trigger-rate wildcard" framing suggested.
>
> The regional dispersion widens through the tail in price terms,
> not in arrears levels. CREA MLS HPI shows national benchmark
> prices down 4.7% Y/Y in March 2026; Toronto's benchmark stood
> at C$944,100 in April 2026, down 6.6% Y/Y; Vancouver was at
> C$1,101,700 in March 2026, down 6.7% Y/Y. Toronto and Vancouver
> are leading the national turn -- in fact, more sharply than the
> national-aggregate read suggests. Negative price momentum
> interacts with renewal stress through the uninsured-LTV
> channel: borrowers whose original LTV was tight have less room
> to refinance into a different product, and the set of available
> products narrows further when the property's mark-to-market
> value has fallen. The arrears-rate response to price weakness
> remains lagged -- the CMHC Toronto projection to 0.34% by Q4
> 2026 is the cleanest published path.
>

## 7. Section V -- What would change our view

> Three triggers fire against this view.
>
> **The wall peaks earlier than this piece claims if**: CMHC's
> mortgage arrears rate (90+ days) stalls or rolls over within
> the next two CMHC RMIR releases (the Spring 2026 vintage is
> not yet released as of 2026-05-11; convention is semi-annual);
> CBA's chartered-bank monthly arrears falls below 0.25% for
> three consecutive months from the current 0.28% reading; and
> Big-Six provisions for credit losses on residential mortgages
> trail below the current C$168M four-quarter writeoff baseline
> in the Q2 FY2026 earnings cycle (mid-2026). The combination
> would say the 2024-2025 stress has begun to dissipate faster
> than the cohort-mechanics model predicts; the tail is
> shorter, not just shallower.
>
> **The wall extends past 2027 if**: CMHC arrears continue to
> rise through 2026H2 and into 2027 without inflecting, with
> the Toronto sub-aggregate moving above 0.40% (above CMHC's own
> Q4 2026 projection of 0.34%); the Toronto MLS HPI Y/Y delta
> pushes below -10% on a cumulative basis (the April 2026
> Toronto reading is already -6.6%, more negative than the
> aggregate of v1 anticipated; the cleaner threshold is -10%,
> consistent with the cumulative peak-to-trough crossing
> approximately 20%); or the BoC 5-year GoC yield moves above
> 4.0%, which would re-widen the renewal-rate spread for the
> 2026H2 and 2027 cohorts beyond the mechanical fade
> described in Section IV.
>
> **We are wrong about the regional-credit framing if**:
> Alberta's CBA arrears rate moves materially above Ontario or
> BC -- specifically, above 0.30% on the monthly series, given
> the current Alberta reading of 0.25% sits essentially at the
> Ontario level (0.26%) and only narrowly above BC (0.21%).
> The current geography says credit-stress levels are Prairie-
> and Atlantic-led (Saskatchewan 0.50%, Manitoba 0.35%,
> Atlantic 0.29%); a clean Alberta breakout would say the
> energy-cycle channel has become the dominant story and the
> renewal wall is no longer the right frame for thinking about
> Canadian residential credit stress through 2027.
>
> The honest answer on the count of renewers is "peaked." The
> honest answer on the depth of the residual tail is "narrower
> than the count suggests, deeper than the aggregates show, and
> regional in two different ways." The cheapest test that would
> resolve the regional framing is the next CMHC RMIR vintage
> (Spring 2026, release date not yet announced), which we will
> mark against here on release. The second-cheapest test is the
> CBA February 2026 PDF direct parse [TK pending researcher
> wave 5], which would confirm the level read on the Prairie
> and Atlantic provinces against the secondary reporting this
> draft relies on.

Voice notes:
- Falsification thresholds reset per TK16: -5% Y/Y HPI was
  already breached in Toronto (-6.6%) and Vancouver (-6.7%),
  so threshold moved to -10%; Alberta-above-ON-or-BC was
  near-tripped (Alberta 0.25% vs ON 0.26%, BC 0.21%), so
  threshold moved to "Alberta materially above ON, e.g.,
  above 0.30%."
- Mode B closing discipline: the closing paragraph is the call
  and the watchpoint.
