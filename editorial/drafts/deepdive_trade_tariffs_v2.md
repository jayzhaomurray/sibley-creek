# Canada's trade pivot that wasn't

**Draft v2 · 2026-05-14**

Canada's share of merchandise exports going to the United States has dropped from 75.4% in March 2024 to 67.2% in March 2026 — an eight-point decline in 24 months that gets read in policy circles as evidence of structural reorientation. The disaggregated data tells a different story. Two specific corridors explain almost the entire shift: gold flowing to London, and aluminum flowing to Rotterdam. Strip those out and the Canadian export base has barely moved.

## Where the headline came from

In the year to March 2026, the United Kingdom's share of Canadian exports rose from 4.7% to 11.4% — a 6.7-point jump that nearly matches the US-share decline. No other partner moved more than one percentage point. India: +0.3. Germany: +0.7. Japan: −0.3. Saudi Arabia: −0.05.

The UK move is gold. Statistics Canada's NAPCS 35 series — unwrought gold, silver, and platinum-group metals — shows Canadian gold exports of C$8.0 billion in March 2026, of which C$7.8 billion (97%) went to the UK. Volume rose roughly 8% year-on-year through September 2025; value rose roughly 58%. The driver is the gold price (USD 4,676 per ounce in May 2026, near record), not flow expansion.

This corridor has existed since 1934, when the Royal Canadian Mint joined the original LBMA Good Delivery List. Canadian gold mined in Ontario and Quebec is refined into 400-ounce Good Delivery bars and physically shipped to London because LBMA accreditation requires it. Counterparties are LBMA member bullion banks — JPMorgan, HSBC, ICBC Standard, UBS — not the Bank of England as a principal buyer. Metal often ends up re-cast in Switzerland for Asian retail demand, but customs records the UK as the destination.

It is a real export. It is not a pivot. It is bullion-market routing amplified by a gold-price cycle.

## The aluminum corridor is real, but narrow and brittle

A second specific flow — Canadian primary aluminum to Rotterdam — does represent genuine reorientation. The architecture is fragile.

Before 2025, roughly 95% of Quebec smelter output went to the United States. After the US imposed a 25% Section 232 tariff on Canadian aluminum in February 2025, doubled to 50% in June, the US share of Quebec exports fell to 78% by Q2 2025. Europe's share rose from 0.2% to 18%. Aluminerie Alouette redirected 57% of its Q2 output to Europe, up from 4%. Alcoa diverted roughly 100,000 tonnes.

The metal lands in the Netherlands not because Dutch industrial demand absorbs it, but because Rotterdam is the port of entry for the Rhine corridor — onward to German automakers, Italian extruders, Belgian and French tier-one suppliers. Some unknown fraction sits in LME-approved warehouses as warranted inventory rather than industrial stock. The Rotterdam P1020A premium collapsed from $320 to $180 per tonne between February and April 2025, confirming real physical absorption.

This is a tariff-arbitrage flow, not a structural reorientation. It works while the 50% rate holds. One bilateral negotiation outcome reverses it.

## The sectoral diagnostic confirms the narrowness

Indexed to January 2020, the 12-month trailing average of non-US exports for the four tariff-exposed sectors reads as follows. Aluminum non-US: 236 — a 2.4× increase from the pre-pandemic baseline. Autos non-US: 115, with the level so small relative to US autos that the absolute pivot is minor. Softwood non-US: 68 — collapse, not pivot. Steel non-US: 82 — the sector shrinking on both sides.

One of the four exposed sectors has materially pivoted. Two are shrinking. One is flat. There is no pattern of broad reorientation underneath the headline number.

## What this means

The structural-reorientation framing implies Canada has options — alternative markets at scale, customers willing to absorb redirected flow, an industrial base that can credibly negotiate from a position of partial independence. The data show none of that. What it shows is a long-standing bullion corridor expanding with the gold price, and a single tariff-driven aluminum diversion that depends on the tariff persisting.

If the negotiation default at the July 2026 USMCA review is extension, both corridors persist. If the tariff stack is softened, the aluminum corridor reverses. Either way, the manufacturing base remains structurally US-dependent. The pivot, on the data we have, is not what it appears to be.
