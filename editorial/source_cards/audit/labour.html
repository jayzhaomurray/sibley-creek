Is the labour market loosening?

Not in May — the headline broke the loosening story. Employment jumped 87,800[1], the fastest gain in a year and a half[2], lifting jobs to their highest level this year — still shy of December's peak[3] — and pulling the unemployment rate down to 6.6% from 6.9%[4]. Slack clears slowly, so it remains the lagging piece, but the cyclical alarm that followed last week's GDP print now reads overdone.

Hiring rebounded across most of the economy in May.

Monthly job change and the unemployment rate
The May surge was broad, not a one-industry fluke: most sectors added jobs[5], and the gains landed in full-time work rather than part-time[6]. Breadth that wide is what separates a real turn from statistical noise. It lifts employment to its highest level this calendar year, though still about 25,000 short of December's peak[7].

02 · Stocks, per-capita

The employed share rose in May, pausing the slide into non-participation.

Labour-force stocks as shares of working-age population
The employed share of the working-age population climbed to 60.7%[8] in May as the unemployed share fell to 4.3%[9]. For much of this cycle, a fast-growing population had been landing outside the labour force rather than in jobs; that absorption paused this month. Participation held at 65.0%[10], so the gain came from filling the existing labour force, not from people stepping away.

03 · Hours vs headcount

Hours are recovering, but still trail the headcount rebound.

Aggregate hours worked vs employment, Y/Y
Hours are recovering, yet they trail the jobs count: aggregate hours are up just 0.3% Y/Y[11] against 0.7% for employment[12]. The gap means firms added bodies faster than they added work[13] for them to do. Until hours catch up, the recovery in headcount overstates how much demand for labour has actually firmed.

04 · Wage band

Wage growth cooled sharply in May, but the cleaner read isn't in yet.

Composition-adjusted vs headline wage growth, Y/Y · Mar 2026 (LFS-Micro) / May 2026 (LFS-all)
Headline wage growth fell to 3.0% Y/Y in May[14], down from 4.5% in April[15]. But the headline mixes raw pay gains with shifts in who is working. The composition-adjusted measure, LFS-Micro, last read 3.1% in March and has no May figure yet[16]. Whether the cooling is genuine or just a change in the workforce awaits the next reading.

05 · Vacancies and slack

A deep-slack regime in the stock of jobs has held for over three years.

Vacancy and unemployment rate over time, plus the spread · Mar 2026
The vacancy rate sat at 2.8% in March[17] against a 6.7% unemployment rate that month[18], leaving vacancies 3.9 percentage points short[19] of the people looking for work. That spread has been negative without a break since November 2022[20] — there are fewer open jobs than jobseekers, and have been for over three years. This is the honest tension with May's hiring pop[21]: flows can turn in a single month, but the standing pool of slack in the job market is still deep.

06 · Flow rates

Layoffs never spiked — the weak spot has been a slow hiring rate.

Job finding and separation rates, monthly transition probabilities · Apr 2026
This was never a layoff problem; the weak spot was always slow hiring—and that side is now turning. Separations held at 2.0% in April[22], near their pre-pandemic norm, as they have all cycle: no spike, ever[23]. The finding rate, meanwhile, jumped to 32.4% from 27.8% in March[24]—the hiring channel thawing before May's +87.8k blowout[25] confirmed it.
