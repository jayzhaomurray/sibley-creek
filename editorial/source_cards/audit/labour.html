Section abstract

Is the labour market loosening, and how fast?

Yes, and the intensive margin is leading. Hours worked are running negative year-over-year while the unemployment rate has drifted up to 6.9%[1], the pattern of a market that is shedding work before it sheds workers — and one where a population surge is being absorbed straight into not-in-labour-force rather than into jobs. Wages are the lagging piece, with composition-adjusted growth at 3.1%[2] still above where the Bank of Canada wants it, but the cyclical direction is no longer in doubt.

01 · Headline jobs print

Employment shrank in April and the jobless rate ticked up.

Monthly job change and the unemployment rate
Canada lost roughly 18,000 jobs in April, the first negative monthly print since February, and the unemployment rate rose to 6.9%[1] from 6.7% in March[2]. April was the third consecutive negative monthly print[3], with the prior negative reading in September 2025[4]. The three-month average has now turned negative as well.

02 · Stocks, per-capita

Slack is showing up as people leaving the labour force, not as rising unemployment.

Labour-force stocks as shares of working-age population · Apr 2026
The employed share of working-age Canada sits at 60.5%[1], the unemployed share at 4.5%[2], and the not-in-labour-force share at 35%[3]. The three shares sum to the full working-age population, and they move in opposite directions over a cycle: when the employed share slips, the slack lands either in unemployment or in not-in-labour-force. Right now it is landing in not-in-labour-force.
Statistics Canada Table 14-10-0287-01 (LFS, monthly SA). Shares derived chart-side: population 15+ equals employment divided by the employment rate; each stock is then expressed as a share of that derived total.

03 · Hours vs headcount

Hours are falling while headcount still grows.

Aggregate hours worked vs employment, Y/Y · Apr 2026
Aggregate hours worked turned year-over-year negative in April at -0.5%[1], while employment is still running modestly positive at +0.3%[2]. The 0.8pp negative spread[3] is the labour market loosening on the intensive margin before it shows up in headcount — shifts get cut before bodies. The spread has been negative for more than twelve months now[4]; April's deepening is the cyclical signal worth watching.
Statistics Canada Tables 14-10-0289-01 (aggregate hours worked, main-job basis, SA) and 14-10-0287-01 (employed persons 15+, SA). Y/Y % change and the hours-minus-employment spread are computed in the chart wrapper.

04 · Wage band

Composition-adjusted wages are running a full point below the headline.

Composition-adjusted vs headline wage growth, Y/Y · Mar 2026 (LFS-Micro) / Apr 2026 (LFS-all)
LFS-Micro, the composition-adjusted series the BoC actually watches, prints 3.1% Y/Y in March[1], up 0.5pp on the month[2]. LFS all-hourly-wages run 4.5% in April[3]: the headline that overstates underlying pressure once who-is-working changes are stripped out. The roughly one-point gap[4] has held since early 2024[5], and the cleaner read is the signal that matters for further easing.
Statistics Canada Table 14-10-0320-02 (LFS hourly wages); BoC LFS-Micro composition-adjusted wage series. LFS-Micro holds the labour-force compositional mix (industry, occupation, age, tenure) constant; LFS-all is the standard 12-month % change in average hourly wages.

05 · Vacancies and slack

The vacancy-unemployment spread sits at -4.1pp in February, deep in slack territory.

Vacancy and unemployment rate over time, plus the spread · Feb 2026
Vacancies ran 2.6% in February[2] against an unemployment rate of 6.7%[3], leaving the spread at -4.1pp[4]. The spread has been negative since October 2022[5], a clean regime shift from the 2022 tight-market window[6] when it briefly ran positive at +0.9 in June[7]. Slack of this depth is consistent with continued disinflation in wage-sensitive categories and gives the BoC room to ease further without reigniting price pressure.
Statistics Canada Table 14-10-0371-01 (JVWS vacancy rate); Statistics Canada Table 14-10-0287-01 (LFS unemployment rate).

06 · Flow rates

Finding rates have softened more than separations have risen — the post-2023 UR drift is a hiring-side story.

Job finding and separation rates, monthly transition probabilities · Mar 2026
Monthly separation rate sits near 2.2% in March[2], only modestly elevated relative to the 2017[3]–19 norm; the job finding rate has slipped to 27.8%[4] from the post-pandemic peak above 35%[5]. The same headline unemployment-rate level can come from rising separations (people losing jobs, leads recessions) or from falling finding rates (matching market frozen, persists into early recoveries). Naming the channel matters for the cycle-stage read.
Statistics Canada Tables 14-10-0287-01 (LFS unemployment level + rate) and 14-10-0342-01 (unemployment by duration); rates derived via Elsby–Michaels–Solon (2009) from stocks + short-duration unemployment.

07 · Regional

Each big province has its own gap to the national rate.

Spread of provincial unemployment rates vs the national rate
Ontario[1] has sat above the national unemployment rate through the past year; Alberta[2] has flipped sides recently; British Columbia[3] ran below Canada for most of the cycle before converging; Quebec[4] has stayed steadily below. Regional dispersion this wide is itself a cycle signal — a national slowdown that is concentrated in central Canada rather than broadly shared. The provincial mix matters for where any policy response would bind first.
