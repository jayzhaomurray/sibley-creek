The per-capita output gap is a denominator story

The headline labour print is flattering. The per-capita series is not. We separate the population-deceleration story from the cyclical weakness story.

Two Canadian labour-market stories have run in parallel since 2023[1]. The headline story is that the economy added jobs through the entire tightening cycle and through the easing cycle that followed: total employment is higher today than at any point before 2024[2]. The per-capita story is that Canadians, on average, are working less and producing less than they were before the pandemic. Real GDP per capita has been below its pre-pandemic trend for eight consecutive quarters as of the Q4 2025⚠ vintage; the employment rate stood at 60.5% in April 2026[35], 2.0 points[36] below the March 2023⚠ cycle peak of 62.5%⚠ and 1.7 points⚠ below the December 2019⚠ baseline of 62.2%[114].

The standard reading is that Canada has a productivity crisis. That reading is partly right and importantly incomplete. Per-capita output is a ratio, and ratios have denominators. From 2022[54] through mid-2024[2], the denominator grew at a pace Canada had not seen in seven decades: total population growth peaked at 3.18%⚠ year-over- year in Q2 2024⚠, almost entirely on non-permanent-resident inflows. The employment base could not keep up arithmetically; per-capita ratios fell even as aggregate employment rose. That mechanic is now running in reverse. The IRCC 2026[11]-2028[20] Immigration Levels Plan, announced in November 2025[75], holds permanent-resident admissions at 380,000 a year and caps temporary-resident arrivals at 385,000 in 2026 and 370,000 in 2027 and 2028[12]. Net non-permanent-resident flow has already been negative for four consecutive quarters. Population growth has fallen from 3.18% YoY in Q2 2024 to -0.25%⚠ YoY in Q1 2026[29].

This piece argues three things. First, the per-capita output gap that opened over 2023[1]-2025[15] was dominated by the population denominator, not by collapsing productivity; Canadian business- sector labour productivity rose 1.1% in 2025[91], with unit-labour-cost growth at 1.2%[17] -- the lowest annual ULC growth since 2017[94][18]. Second, that denominator is now turning fast: the IRCC 2026-2028 Immigration Levels Plan and the four-quarter net non-permanent-resident contraction[19] already in the data set the trajectory of the per-capita denominator through 2028. Third, under any reasonable set of assumptions, the per-capita employment ratio retraces a meaningful share of its 2023-2026 decline on population arithmetic alone, before any hiring pickup.

None of that vacates the productivity question. Canada's per-hour output remains roughly a fifth below the United States and has not closed the gap meaningfully in two decades; that is the structural fight. It is a different fight from the one the per-capita-GDP series was running over 2024 and 2025[23]. The data have changed faster than the framing.

---

The divergence

The employment rate peaked at 62.5% in March 2023[32] and has trended down steadily: 61.3% by June 2024[33], 60.9% by June 2025[34], and 60.5%⚠ in April 2026. By April 2026 it stood 2.0 points below the cycle peak and 1.7 points below the pre-pandemic December 2019⚠ baseline of 62.2% -- a deeper gap than the headline unemployment rate of 6.9%[38] suggested. The participation rate followed a similar arc, peaking at 65.9% in June 2023[39] and falling to 65.0% by April 2026[40], 90 bps below cycle peak[41].

Statistics Canada's Q4 2025 release, published February 27, 2026[42], showed per-capita real GDP unchanged Q/Q[43] after a +0.5% Q3 2025[44], with aggregate real GDP for 2025 at +1.7%[45] -- the slowest annual print outside 2020[46]. Total employment kept rising through both the tightening and easing cycles. By the per-capita measure, the average Canadian was producing less in 2025 than in 2019[47]. By the aggregate measure, Canada was growing.

Both readings are correct. They diverge because the population base they share grew at a pace Canada had not run in seven decades. The Quarterly Population Estimates (Table 17-10-0009)[48] put total population YoY growth at 3.18% in Q2 2024[49] -- a peak higher than anything in the post-war series outside the immediate post-war years themselves. The labour market could keep adding jobs in aggregate and still lose ground per person, because per-person is a ratio against a denominator running ahead of any plausible hiring pace.

---

The denominator turns

The denominator is no longer running ahead. YoY total population growth printed 0.94% in Q3 2025[51] and -0.25% in Q1 2026[52] -- the first multi-quarter outright contraction in the post-war series outside emigration anomalies. Net non-permanent-resident flow, the component that drove the 2022-2024 surge[54], has printed negative for four consecutive quarters: -55k in Q1 2025[55], -59k in Q2[56], -176k in Q3[57], and -171k in Q4[58]. The composition of Canadian population growth has inverted within eighteen months[59].

The IRCC 2026-2028 Immigration Levels Plan, announced in November[60] 2025, sets the framework[61]. Permanent-resident admissions are stabilized at 380,000 a year across 2026-2028[62] -- below the 500,000 path under the previous plan but above the pre-pandemic baseline of roughly 330,000. Temporary-resident arrivals are capped at 385,000 in 2026 and 370,000 in 2027-2028[65], with sub-caps of 155,000 international students in 2026 and 150,000 in 2027-28⚠, and 230,000 temporary workers in 2026 and 220,000 in 2027⚠-28. The stated objective is "to return to sustainable immigration levels through continued decreases to temporary resident arrivals and stabilized permanent resident admissions." The plan sets the trajectory of the per-capita denominator through the end of 2028[68].

The Bank of Canada has taken the framework on board. The April 2026⚠ Monetary Policy Report notes that "population growth slowdown is constraining demand and weighing on housing activity" and that potential output growth is expected to slow in 2026 and recover in 2027-2028 as the immigration trajectory normalizes. Bank of Canada SAN 2025-14[71] decomposes the labour-input contribution to potential growth and shows how sensitive the medium-term path is to working-age population growth specifically.

The arithmetic of the closing gap is straightforward. If population growth runs at roughly 0.3-0.6% in 2026-2027 (consistent with the IRCC plan and the net non-permanent-resident contraction already underway) and employment grows even modestly -- say 0.5-1.0% over the same window -- the employment rate rises mechanically by roughly 0.6 to 0.9 points[78] over the next six quarters[79]. That alone closes roughly half of the 1.7-point⚠ gap between April 2026's 60.5% reading and the pre-COVID 62.2% baseline. No hiring boom, no investment renaissance, no productivity inflection required. The plan vintage is the load- bearing forward variable.

The labour market has loosened but not broken. The job vacancy rate has fallen to 2.6%[17] as of February 2026⚠ (StatCan Table 14-10-0325) -- below the 2019 average of roughly 3.2%[83] and well off the 6.0% April 2022 peak[84]. EI regular beneficiaries sit at 542,000 in February 2026, off November 2025's 568,000[86] but well above the 400,000 lows of 2022[87]. Headline unemployment at 6.9%[88] sits modestly above the early-2024 5.8% reading[89]. The cyclical loosening is real but the per-capita ratio's deeper move is a denominator story running alongside it.

---

Productivity, properly placed

Per-capita GDP decomposes algebraically into three terms: GDP-per- hour (labour productivity), hours-per-employed-person (intensive margin), and the employment-population ratio (extensive margin). The standard popular framing assigns the bulk of Canadian per-capita weakness to the first term, on the strength of the well-known long-running productivity gap with the United States. The 2023-2025 decomposition[90] does not support that assignment.

Labour productivity in the Canadian business sector rose 1.1%[77] in 2025, per the Statistics Canada productivity release of March 4, 2026; over the same year, unit labour costs grew 1.2%[93] -- the lowest annual ULC growth since 2017[18]. The quarterly print for Q4 2025⚠ was -0.1%[96] Q/Q on business-sector productivity, with hours worked also -0.1% Q/Q and unit labour costs +0.7% Q/Q[97] -- softer, but well off the negative readings of 2022-2023[98]. Over the full 2023-2025 window, GDP-per-hour was not the term that pulled per-capita output below trend; the employment-population ratio was.

Bank of Canada Staff Discussion Paper 2025-8, published in May 2025⚠, sharpens the point. The paper documents that a material share of the measured divergence in wage growth between non-permanent residents and Canadian-born workers reflects compositional change in the workforce mix. The implication for productivity measurement is direct: a fraction of what looked like productivity weakness in 2022-2024 was a composition effect[101] from a rapid shift toward lower- experience, lower-tenure non-permanent-resident workers in lower- output-per-hour roles. As that shift reverses, measured productivity should improve mechanically -- and the 2025 +1.1% productivity print is consistent with that improvement having begun.

The structural Canadian productivity gap remains. Canadian business- sector productivity is roughly a fifth below US levels and has not closed meaningfully in two decades; the literature on capital deepening, R&D intensity, and the small-firm productivity wedge points to slow-moving structural causes policy has visibly struggled to address. That fight is real, and multi-decade. Reading the 2023-2025 per-capita-GDP series[110] as the productivity-crisis signal it has often been claimed to be overstates what the series tells us. The denominator did most of the work.

Where the per-capita employment rate lands in 2027

The arithmetic, framed as a scenario not a forecast. Holding employment at April 2026 levels[111] and letting population grow at 0.3-0.6% through end-2027[112] -- a path consistent with the IRCC plan and the net non-permanent-resident contraction already in the data -- lands the employment rate at 61.1-61.4%[113], closing roughly half the gap to the pre-COVID 62.2% baseline. No hiring boom, no investment renaissance, no productivity inflection required. Any cyclical recovery on top of that lands the ratio at or modestly above pre-COVID.

Under any reasonable parameterization of the inputs that are now reasonably well-defined -- the IRCC plan, the observed net non-permanent-resident contraction, a labour market that loosens but does not break -- the per-capita employment ratio retraces a material share of its 2023-2026 decline within six quarters[118]. The headline-versus-per- capita divergence is a 2024-2025 story[119] that is mechanically closing on its own.

---

What would change our mind

If prime-age (25-54) participation falls below 87.5%[120] on a three- month moving-average basis (currently 88.5%)[121], then the framing here is wrong. A move of that size would say labour-market softening has begun to detach prime-age workers from job search. The forward path becomes considerably worse than the scenarios imply.

If the IRCC 2026-2028 plan is revised upward[122] in either sub-cap before the next plan vintage (2027-2029⚠, conventionally announced November 2026⚠), then the denominator-closes-the-gap mechanic weakens proportionally. The plan vintage is the forward variable this turns on; if the plan changes, the arithmetic changes with it.

If labour productivity prints two consecutive negative quarterly Q/Q readings after the 2025 annual recovery, then the composition- effect reversal documented in BoC SDP 2025-8[125] is being overwhelmed by something else, and the productivity question moves back toward the centre of the per-capita story. The next two prints (Q1 2026[29], expected June 2026⚠; Q2 2026⚠, expected September 2026⚠) are the cheapest test.

The single cheapest test of the central thesis is the next Quarterly Population Estimates release (Table 17-10-0009, Q2 2026 vintage, expected late September 2026). If the population contraction observed in Q4 2025 and Q1 2026[128] reverses sharply -- back above +1.5%[77] YoY -- the IRCC plan is not binding the way the denominator argument claims. If the series continues at or near zero YoY, the mechanic is doing exactly what the plan vintage implies.
