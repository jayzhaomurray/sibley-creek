Canada's trade pivot that wasn't

Strip out gold and most of the apparent pivot is gone. Among the tariffed sectors, only aluminum managed to meaningfully diversify. Autos haven't dodged the hit at all.

In March 2026⚠, 66.1%[1] of Canada's merchandise exports went to the United States. In calendar 2024, the figure averaged 76.3%[2]. The share has fallen roughly ten percentage points in fourteen months[3].

It is tempting to read that as a collapse. It is not. Total Canadian merchandise exports hit a record CAD 68.8 billion⚠ in March 2026[19], well above the pre-tariff baseline near CAD 61 billion[5]. The trade balance with the United States widened in Canada's favour to CAD 7.1 billion[6]. The overall trade balance flipped from a CAD 2.4 billion deficit a year earlier[7] to a CAD 1.8 billion[8] surplus. Exports did not shrink. Their geography moved.

That movement is the largest peacetime reorientation of Canadian trade since the late-1980s free-trade negotiations[64]. Most of the public argument is still pointed at the July 1 USMCA joint review as if it were the decisive event. The decisive event is already behind us. The question now is whether the reorientation consolidates or partially unwinds.

This piece argues that the reorientation is the central fact of Canadian macro for 2026[62], and that the durable policy threat running underneath it is not the IEEPA tariff regime imposed in February 2025[63] -- it is the Section 232 stack on steel, aluminum, copper, autos, and softwood lumber. That stack is sector-targeted, legally distinct from IEEPA, and operating today.

---

What actually escalated

Two tracks ran in parallel through 2025[42]. The first is the IEEPA track. On 2025-02-01, the United States invoked the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose 25% tariffs on non-CUSMA- compliant Canadian goods and 10% on energy and potash, effective 2025-02-04[10]. On 2025-08-01, the non-compliant rate rose to 35%[11]. Approximately 98% of Canada-US tariff lines and 99.9% of bilateral trade qualify as CUSMA-compliant[12] when documentation is prepared, so the IEEPA rates bind on a narrower base than the headlines suggested.

The second track is Section 232 -- the national-security tariff authority -- and it is the one that matters.

  • Steel: raised from 25% to 50% on 2025-06-04[13].
  • Aluminum: raised from 25% to 50% on 2025-06-04[14].
  • Copper: 50% on core articles and 25% on derivatives, added

2026-04-06 under a 2026-04-02 proclamation[15].

  • Autos and auto parts: 25%[16].
  • Softwood lumber: 10% Section 232 stacked on top of the existing

countervailing and anti-dumping duties, effective October 2025[17].

A de minimis carve-out arrived in April 2026: goods containing 15% or less subject metal content are exempt[18]. A relief path also opened that month allowing Canadian and Mexican producers to qualify for reduced Section 232 rates by committing to build primary capacity inside the United States. Both are narrow.

!What's actually in force Tariff regimes by sector and authority, current as of 2026-05-11[19]. Rows: energy, autos, steel, aluminum, copper, softwood lumber, dairy, other. Columns: IEEPA non-compliant rate, Section 232 rate, AD/CVD. Source: US Federal Register, Congressional Research Service IN12519, US Commerce Department proclamations.

The Section 232 stack is the durable threat for three reasons. It rests on a different statutory authority than IEEPA. It is sector-targeted rather than country-targeted, which gives it constituencies inside the United States, not just opponents. And it operates today, with rates already at 50% on the metals lines and a stacked 45% effective burden on softwood lumber[20].

---

The softwood arithmetic

Softwood is the sharpest illustration of how the two tariff systems stack. The anti-dumping and countervailing duty rate on Canadian softwood sat at a combined 35.16% before April 2026[21]. The Section 232 rate on lumber adds another 10%[22]. The effective burden today is approximately 45%[23].

A preliminary Commerce Department review published on 2026-04-14 proposes cutting the AD/CVD components to 24.83% (anti-dumping 10.66% plus countervailing 14.17%)[24], pending final determinations in August and October 2026[25]. If those preliminary cuts hold, the effective burden falls to about 34.83%[26] -- still high, still binding, and still entirely separate from anything USMCA can fix on July 1.

!Lumber stack Softwood lumber effective tariff stack -- AD plus CVD plus Section 232 -- before and after the April 2026⚠ preliminary review. Source: US Federal Register notice 2026-07154 (2026-04-14⚠), US Commerce Department, Congressional Research Service R48781.

Even a "win" in the softwood review leaves British Columbia mills facing a tariff burden equivalent to roughly a third of cargo value. The metals and auto tracks sit higher than the post-cut lumber rate.

---

The reorientation, decomposed

The US export share moved from 76.3% to 66.1% in fourteen months[27]. Three forces are mixing in roughly unknown proportions: genuine demand diversion to Europe, Asia, and other non-US destinations; the unwind of front-loading that pulled US-bound shipments into early 2025 ahead of expected tariff escalations and is now reversing in Q1 2026[28]; and currency effects on the CAD denomination of US-bound flows.

A clean decomposition requires commodity-by-destination detail from Statistics Canada Table 12-10-0011 not yet pulled in raw form. The aggregate evidence is enough to make the structural point: total exports rose to a record while US share fell. That combination rules out the simplest reading -- that US tariffs caused a Canadian export contraction. They caused a redirection.

!Total exports rose, US share fell Total Canadian merchandise exports (bars, left axis, CAD billions) and US share (line, right axis, percent), monthly, January 2023 to March 2026[29]. Annotations mark IEEPA imposition (February 2025) and Section 232 escalations (June 2025)[30]. Source: Statistics Canada international merchandise trade, customs basis, seasonally adjusted.

The open question is how much of the diverted volume is "sticky" -- new commercial relationships that persist if US tariffs ease versus opportunistic spot trade that reverses on the first sign of relief. That is the central uncertainty in any 2026-H2 forecast[65]. The working assumption here is partial unwind: from 66-70% in Q1 2026 toward 70-72% by year-end[31] if no new escalation arrives. That is inference, not data.

---

The Bank of Canada's calibration -- and what it implicitly assumes

The most-cited macro pass-through estimate is the Bank of Canada's January 2025 scenario: a permanent 25% US tariff on all imports including Canada, plus 25% Canadian retaliation[32]. The benchmark output is Canadian GDP growth in Year 1 roughly 2.5 percentage points lower than the counterfactual path[33]. A 2% baseline becomes -0.5%; a 0.5% Year 2 follows[34]. Tariff pass-through to consumer prices is initially low and builds toward full pass-through over three years[35].

The scenario was calibrated to a broad, symmetric, country-level shock. What actually arrived is a stack of sectoral Section 232 tariffs plus a narrow IEEPA regime that binds on a small share of trade. The realized shape is asymmetric, not the broad shock the scenario modelled.

That matters for two reasons. First, the realized GDP drag should come in below the 2.5-percentage-point scenario, because the base is narrower. The Bank's July 2025 update noted observed tariff effects on inflation had been limited to date, with core inflation rising from roughly 2% in late 2024 to about 2.5% by June 2025, mostly in non-energy goods[36]. That is consistent with narrower-than- modelled pass-through.

Second, the political-economy distribution is the opposite of diffuse. A 50% aluminum tariff[37] that adds a small share of a point to national GDP can take a significant chunk out of the Quebec aluminum corridor. A 25% auto tariff[38] aims at Ontario. The 45% lumber stack[39] aims at British Columbia. The national-aggregate framing understates the provincial concentration, and that concentration is where the policy pressure on the federal response will come from.

!Scenario versus realized Bank of Canada January 2025 Monetary Policy Report tariff scenario (benchmark path)[40] versus realized Canadian GDP growth, quarterly, 2025-Q1 through 2026-Q1[41]. Source: Bank of Canada MPR 2025-01-29[42] In-Focus 1; Statistics Canada Table 36-10-0104.

Governor Macklem's February 2025 speech "Tariffs, structural change and monetary policy"[43] set the reaction-function framework. Tariffs are a supply-side shock. The Bank looks through first- round price-level effects and responds only if inflation expectations de-anchor or wage-price dynamics emerge. The Bank has since presented scenario analysis rather than point forecasts in its MPRs -- the first time it has done so since COVID[44] -- because the tariff path is unknowable.

---

USMCA, July 1, and what the review actually is

The market narrative treats the USMCA joint review on 2026-07-01[45] as a renegotiation flashpoint. It is closer to the opposite.

Article 34.7 of USMCA sets a sixth-anniversary joint review on 2026-07-01[46]. Each of the three parties must confirm in writing whether to extend the agreement's sixteen-year term[47]. If all three confirm, the agreement runs to 2042[48]. If any one party objects, the agreement enters annual review mode with a sunset trajectory to 2036[49]. Recommendations for substantive changes must be submitted at least one month before the review, meaning the practical deadline is 2026-06-01[50].

The mechanism's default is extension. Substantive renegotiation requires unanimity; a single party declining to confirm does not automatically reopen the text -- it triggers a slower review process. The structural bias is toward continuation.

That is not the same thing as saying renegotiation will not happen. The provisions widely flagged as in play include auto rules of origin, Chapter 31 dispute settlement, dairy tariff-rate quotas, digital trade carve-outs, and anti-circumvention. The review is a real policy venue. But the binary "USMCA collapses on July 1" framing common in market commentary is structurally unlikely.

!USMCA review calendar USMCA joint review calendar -- September 2025 USTR consultations open[51], 2026-06-01 recommendations deadline[52], 2026-07-01 joint review trigger[53], confirmation deadline. Source: Congressional Research Service R48787 and IF10997[54]; US Federal Register notice 2025-18010 (2025-09-17)[55].

July 1 is not the decisive event in the Canadian tariff story. The Section 232 stack is the decisive policy, and it sits outside USMCA's scope entirely. USMCA can be renewed cleanly on July 1 and the steel, aluminum, copper, auto, and lumber tariffs will still be in force on July 2.

---

What would change our mind

The argument rests on three facts: a ten-point US share drop in fourteen months[56], a Section 232 stack operating on five sectors today, and a USMCA review whose default is extension. Three things would falsify the reading.

A sharp reversal of the US share decline. If Q2 and Q3 2026 prints take US share back above 75%[57] with no corresponding tariff de- escalation, the "structural reorientation" framing is wrong and front-loading distortions were the story all along. The August and October 2026 trade prints[58] will settle it.

A Section 232 climb-down -- meaningful rollback of the 50% metals tariffs or the 25% auto rate[59] through the new investment-based relief path or bilateral negotiation. If the durable channel softens materially, the macro pass-through mostly evaporates and the BoC scenario calibration becomes a ceiling rather than a benchmark.

A USMCA fracture on July 1 -- one of the three parties declining to confirm extension. Default mechanics make that unlikely but not impossible. A fracture would shift average Canada-US trade toward most-favoured-nation tariff schedules over time, with the highest peaks in sectors USMCA had been quietly protecting.

The single print to watch hardest is the May and June 2026 trade releases[60], for the share trajectory and for any commodity-level evidence on where the diverted volumes are going. The single date to watch hardest is 2026-07-01[61].
